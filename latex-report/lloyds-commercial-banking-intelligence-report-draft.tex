\chapter*{Abstract}
% Compulsory, at most 1 page. Owner: TBC, written LAST.
\note{One page. The problem (Lloyds needs to find BB/SME prospects and risks

A commercial bank has full visibility of its own customers and very little of companies it does not serve. Working with Lloyds Banking Group's Commercial Banking division, this project asks whether the public record alone can identify business and SME prospects and risks among both its existing customers and companies that are not yet clients, using only freely available data and no internal information. The question is framed as a search over a large and sparse population rather than a classification over a known customer base and is scoped to roughly 1.4 mln SMEs across three target sectors.

To answer it, we reconstructed a history the register does not provide, assembling thirty-three monthly snapshots of CH into a company month panel of 49.6 mln rows. On this panel we engineered 41 features across four families and defined four self-labelled targets, each read from a future state of the same register so that the labels are learnable without lookahead. We then compared an interpretable baseline against a gradient-boosted tree and a neural network over eleven recorded model runs, and attached a SHAP explanation to every score, so that the output is a ranked and defensible shortlist with a reason for each entry.

The project's central finding is that the structured public record does carry predictive signal, with the lending readiness shortlist ranking companies at roughly one hundred times the base rate among its top candidates. 

Unstructured news, articles and media, however, do not reach companies of this size at all, and when its coverage of the target population was measured, it was negligible, so unstructured signals were added for companies flagged with signals. The statutory record of insolvency (the Gazette) does reach these companies, but only once they have already failed, by which point a lender has nothing left to act on. Overall, mapping these limits is a contribution in itself: knowing which sources can predict and which can only confirm lets us concentrate on the public records that carry a usable signal instead of the ones that arrive too late.

\noindent
In quantitative terms the project delivered the following
\begin{itemize}
    \item a 33-month company-month panel of 49.6 mln rows over the CH register;
    \item 41 engineered features across four families and four self-labelled prediction targets;
    \item an eleven-run model comparison across three model families;
    \item a 25.4-hour programmatic harvest of 558{,}693 secured charges;
    \item a 284{,}230 notice crawl of \emph{The Gazette};
    \item an interactive HTML and CSS-based dashboard with global and single-firm views, pre-coded filterings and a market analysis 
\end{itemize}

LBG's Commercial Banking division provides lending, transactional banking, working-capital management, debt financing and risk-management services to business \cite{lloyds_products}, operating through LBG and its family of brands \cite{lbg_brands}. This project, carried out by our team in collaboration with the bank, asks whether the public record alone, including CH and other free sources, can identify commercial opportunities and risks among both existing customers and companies the bank currently does not serve, without any access to internal client data.

\section{The problem and the research question}
\label{sec:problem}
A commercial bank has complete visibility of its own customers and very little of those it does not serve, so the task is to identify, among roughly 5.7 mln companies on the UK register \cite{companieshouse_api}, those likely to need lending, those showing early signs of distress and growth opportunities, explicitly including companies that do not bank with Lloyds. This is therefore a search problem over a large and highly sparse population, in which the events of interest are rare, and most of the population is invisible to the bank today, rather than a classification problem over a known customer base. To make this tractable, we scoped the analysis to three sectors: Technology, Legal \& Professional; Manufacturing and Fast growth \& emerging. This reduces the register to roughly 1.5 mln companies. These sectors were chosen because they were expected to have a high proportion of SMEs rather than large corporates, since SMEs are the segment where the bank's visibility gap is widest and its commercial interest greatest. 

This scoping restricts the population by sector, but not by size. This means the signal that separates smaller firms from larger ones, remains available to the model. However. We achieve SME focus as a lens at the point of use: the delivered dashboard lets a RM filter the scored population down to SMEs (or any size band). This keeps the model's training and ranking population consistent with the register it was built from, while still letting the end user work the segment the brief cares about most.

The central question of this project is:
\begin{quote}
\itshape
Can the public record of strucured and unstructured data sources alone, with no access to internal bank data, be mined to rank companies in these sectors by their likely need for commercial banking services, including companies the bank does not currently serve, and can each ranking be explained well enough to act on?
\end{quote}

\section{Why it matters, and to whom}
\note{Three named audiences from the client brief: the Growth team (``not
ours''), the Attrition team (``ours and leaving''), the Maintenance team
(``ours and staying''). Say here that those three are defined by
\emph{counterparty}, not by need, which is why counterparty identity became a
workstream of its own and why the routing in \S\ref{sec:routing} is the piece
that turns a ranked list into something a person can act on.}

Small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) account for the majority of the UK businesses and employment \cite{beis_bpe}. Yet, they face persistent gaps in access to finance, which makes their banking relationships both commercially valuable and contested \cite{bbb_2024,oecd_2024_sme}. A bank that predicts need can earn two advantages: it can approach a prospect before a competitor does, and it can flag a risk to an existing client early enough to act. Both matter because a bank has little visibility of companies it does not serve and because early signs of change frequently appear in public filings before they appear anywhere internal. The project is carried out on an established idea that public and alternative data carry genuine value for financial outcomes. Corporate failure prediction from public financial signals is long studied  \cite{altman_1968}, and recent work shows non-traditional, digital signals can predict credit outcomes competitively \cite{berg_etal_2020}. Our contribution is to apply this idea to a specific commercial banking framing of growth, deepening existing relationships and attrition, using only free public data. We explain every model with SHAP \cite{lundberg_lee_2017}. The target users are the division's RMs, who gain an evidence-led shortlist with a reason for each potential clients.

The brief is framed around three functions which we carefully extracted from the Commercial Banking team's stated mission. The growth function focuses on companies that are not already Lloyds clients. Attrition focuses on existing clients who may be leaving Lloyds. Maintenance focuses on retaining existing customers. These groups are distinguished by counterparty, meaning which bank already serves the company and not by the company's need; the same company can be an opportunity for one team and risk for another depending on who currently holds its lending. So, one single ranked list of likely borrowers cannot support all three priorities. This is why identifying each company's counterparty, using CH charges API \cite{companieshouse_api}, became a workstream and why the dashboard was structured to view companies across these three buckets.

\section {Scope: data sources and the structured-first pipeline}
As a team we explored a broad range of free public sources beyond the brief's suggestion including the CH register and its APIs \cite{companieshouse_api}, public sector contract awards from Contracts Finder and Find a Tender \cite{contractsfinder,findatender,ocds}, secured lender identity from CH charges API \cite{companieshouse_api}, hiring, property and intellectual property signals \cite{adzuna_api, landregistry_ccod,ukipo_tm}, and unstructured news and sentiment \cite{gdelt, newsapi,guardian_api,gnews_api}. This pipeline is structured data first, including structured signals and shortlisted companies, and unstructured media is retrieved only for companies a signal has already flagged, keeping an expensive and sparse lookup targeted rather than run across the whole population.

\section{Central challenges and how we mitigated them}

Four challenges were important to solve:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Coverage versus depth:} The bulk file covers every company but is shallow, while the APIs are rich but rate-limited. So the cheap, population-wide features had to come from the bulk file, and the deeper, per-company detail had to be reserved for a shortlist. We engineered the population features straight from the bulk file at no cost. For most of the expensive API work we ran it on a sample rather than across the whole population. The exception was the lender identity behind each mortgage charge, which the client's growth and attrition questions depend on directly: here, rather than sampling, we ran the harvest to completion across every company with an outstanding charge, staying within the CH limit of about two requests per second, retrying automatically on errors, and caching every response. This took about 25 hours (\S\ref{sec:charges}) but returned the full dataset, because we did not want to compromise on coverage \cite{companieshouse_api}.
    \item \textbf{Choosing what to predict:} CH records a company's status only as it stands now, not the date it changed, which makes a clean target hard to define and creates a leakage risk when the label and the features are drawn from the same moment so that a model can learn from after-effects of failure rather than early warning signs. We avoided this by taking every label from a later month than its features: features from month $t$, labels from month $t{+}1$ onwards (\S\ref{sec:targets}). This way the model only ever learns from the past to predict the future. We then ran an automated test to confirm that no feature secretly reached into the future; the leakage defect this discipline later exposed, and its repair, are the subject of \S\ref{sec:leak}.
    \item \textbf{Relationship gap:} Public data describes the company, not its banking relatinship. We can estimate need, but public data alone cannot tell us which bank already serves the company. To narrow this gap, we recovered counterparty identity from CH charges API by reading \texttt{persons\_entitled} lender on each charge and classifying it as Lloyds brand or a competitor using auditable rule dictionary \cite{companieshouse_api} (\S\ref{sec:charges}). This is the closest proxy for the banking relationship and allowed us to let the shortlisth be routed to the client's three relationships (\S\ref{sec:routing}).
    \item \textbf{Reliable linkage:} Matching a record to the correct company is error-prone, so we joined on the CH number only, the  \texttt{GB-COH}, padded to a common key and never on company name. A name and postcode match was added as a separate, precision-tested extension (\S\ref{sec:namematch}). We attempted unstructured enrichment only on a small, justified shortlist to keep the risk of mislinkage low.

\end{itemize}

% 6 pages. Owner: Sneha, Viktor, Sam

\section{Literature on predicting corporate distress and corporate demand}
The classical approach to predicting company failure is built on financial ratios. The best-known example is Altman's Z-score \cite{altman_1968}, which combines accounting ratios into a single score placing a firm in a 'safe', 'grey' or 'distress' zone. It endures because it is simple, interpretable and computed entirely from published accounts. That last property is also its limitation. The Z-score assumes full, comparable accounts are on file, but SMEs, the bulk of the UK register, are entitled to file abbreviated or 'micro-entity' accounts: a balance sheet and short notes, with no profit-and-loss statement \cite{companieshouse_accounts_2026}. For these firms the ratios a Z-score needs are missing or too coarse to use, and it is exactly these firms our brief centres on. The Z-score nevertheless sets three expectations we carry forward. 

Predicting distress is a legitimate, long-established target, and our Credit Risk Exposure target (\S\ref{sec:targets}) is a form of it. Interpretability is not optional: like the Z-score, every score we produce comes with the factors behind it, through SHAP rather than fixed coefficients. And because the financial ratios the Z-score relies on are unavailable at SME scale, our contribution is to reconstruct predictive signal from what the register does expose rather than from accounts like Altman does.

A second line of work responds to this gap by turning to non-financial and behavioural information. Altman, Sabato and Wilson \cite{altman_sabato_wilson_2010} show that non-financial signals materially improve SME default prediction over accounts-only models, and later credit scoring research finds that behavioural and transactional data can perform as well as or even better than traditional financial variables \cite{berg_etal_2020}. This motivates our own emphasis on register-derived behaviour such as charges, director changes, and filing activity rather than on reported financial data alone.

Almost all of this literature predicts distress: which firms will fail. The complementary question about which firms are about to need finance and are ready to take it on is far less studied, even though it is the one a commercial lender most wants answered. This demand-side framing is what sets our project apart from conventional default models.

\section{The modelling background}
\label{sec:modelling-backgroud}
\note{Why gradient-boosted trees on tabular data with heavy missingness; why we
rank rather than classify (a RM works a list, not a decision
boundary), which makes ROC-AUC and P@$N$ the right pair of metrics and
accuracy the wrong one; and SHAP, both what it computes and why we need it, since
a shortlist without a reason attached to each row cannot be acted on. Keep the
maths light but present: an examiner should be able to read the SHAP figures.}

Here we outline the modelling choiches that shape everything later reported in Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}; each of them follows from a property of the data: the model family, the framing of the task as ranking, and the requirement that every score comes with a reason attached.

\subsection{The model familes we used}
What we assemble in Chapter~\ref{chap:execution} is a wide tabular matrix of roughly 40 columns per company-month: counts, ratios, boolean flags and two categorical fields, with a great deal of missingness that is informative rather than accidental. A NULL twelve-month delta means the company was not on the register a year earlier; a NULL recency means the event has never happened at all. Gradient-boosted decision trees \cite{4a848dd1-54e3-3c3c-83c3-04977ded2e71, ke2017lightgbm} are the natural fit for exactly this shape. These models build up a series of small decision trees, each one correcting the errors of the trees before it. Three features mattered for us:
\begin{itemize}
    \item They split on rank, not size, so a £150 billion procurement ceiling counts simply as "large" rather than a distorting outlier.
    \item They need no scaling or manual encoding.
    \item They send missing values down a learned branch, so "we can't compute this" becomes usable information rather than a gap to be patched with an average the company doesn't have.
\end{itemize}
   The alternatives struggle on these same points. A regularised linear model can't capture interactions like the one between a size band and a charge count, and it needs missing values filled in, asserting something about a company our data doesn't support. A neural network would have to learn the mixed data types, the missingness convention, and the sharp regulatory thresholds from scratch, off a few hundred thousand rows.

   We decided to fit both alternatives anyway: the linear model as an interpretable baseline and the network as the natural representative of the complex end of the interpretability trade-off. Their role is to provide comparison, not competition.

\subsection{The task is ranking, not classification}
A RM works a finite call list, not a decision boundary. We were never asked to sort companies into yes and no, so we do not: we rank them by predicted probability and hand over the top of the list.

That choice also rules out the obvious metric. At a base rate near one in three hundred, a model that labels every company "no" is over ninety-nine per cent accurate and completely useless, so accuracy here is not just weak but misleading. The same objection sinks any single-threshold statistic, such as
recall or F1, unless the threshold is separately justified. We therefore report two threshold-free metrics that answer different questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item ROC-AUC \cite{FAWCETT2006861} tests the ordering globally: draw a positive and a negative at random, how often is the positive ranked above the negative.
    \item P@$N$ \cite{manning2008introduction} tests the top of the list: of the $N$ companies we actually put in front of someone for a specific target, how many were right.
\end{itemize}
A model can look fine on the first and fail the second, so keeping both, at several values of $N$, is cheap insurance; the divergence between them is what exposed a leakage defect in our own results within a minute of reading the table (\S\ref{sec:leak}). Finally, P@$N$ must be read against its base rate to adjust its predictive power, we develop this further in Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}.

\subsection{SHAP for score explanation}
\label{subsec:shap-background}
A ranked list is not yet a deliverable: the person receiving it has to open a conversation on why the company ranked high. We therefore attach per-company attributions using SHAP \cite{lundberg_lee_2017}, which adapts the Shapley value from cooperative game theory \cite{shapley1953value}. The features of a single row are treated as players in a game whose payout is that row's prediction, and each feature is credited with its average marginal contribution over all orderings in which the players could have arrived. The property we rely on is that these contributions sum exactly to the prediction, so a score can be broken down into what drove it rather than what merely correlates with it. The cost of computing this number differs sharply across the three model families. A linear model needs no explainer at all: the attribution reduces to a rescaling of coefficients already printed on the fitted model, so we read ours straight from those. For the boosted tree ensemble, \texttt{TreeExplainer} \cite{lundberg_etal_2020} uses the tree structure to compute exact values in seconds for a hundred thousand rows. Finally, a dense network has no shortcut at all; the model-agnostic \texttt{KernelExplainer} repeatedly perturbs each row and fits a local surrogate, at roughly a thousand times the cost and with sampling error of its own. We use SHAP rather than LIME \cite{ribeiro2016whyitrustyou} because for trees it is exact rather than a surrogate fitted to sampled neighbours, and because it is stable across re-runs, which matters when the same company may be shown to the same person twice. Finally, every metric here is a single point estimate from one held-out sample. Nothing in this project is reported without a confidence interval, and \S\ref{sec:errors} sets out what those intervals cover, which differs by model family.

\section{Public data sources beyond the register}
The register only shows what a company has officially submitted and does not show the activites a company has carried out. If a business purchases a warehouse, secures a local authority contract, registers a trademark or receives public funding for research, this will leave evidence in other public databases and these records are not included in the CH bulk file. In this section we review the external sources we used to find this additional information.

% Each source identifies a company in either one of two main ways and the difference between this affects the quality of the results strongly as discussed in \S\ref{sec:sources-eval}. Some sources provide the CH registration number, meaning that we can match the companies directly using exact string matching after both values are formatted as eight characters. The other sources only provide a company name, meaning that we must identify the company through name matching, which is a probability based process rather than a simple lookup. For example, an Oxford compay named "Amazon Ltd" is not necessarily connected to Amazon Web Services. Adding details such as postcodes can improve matching the accuracy but it does not exactly guarantee the correct results, especially when those details are incomplete.

These sources fall into two groups, and which group a source belongs to governs how reliably its records can be attched to a company. The first group publishes the CH registration number alongside each record, so a company can be identified by a direct lookup. HM Land Registry's Commercial and Corporate Data \cite{landregistry_ccod} reports the registered owners of freehold and leashold property in England and Wales in this form, and property held on a comapany's own account is a durable indication of the assets taht a secured lending decision rests on. Contracts Finder and Find a Tender publish public sector contract awards through OCDS feeds and carry supplier indentifiers often enough to support relaible matching. The Gazette prints the company numebr inside the text of each insolvency notice, so notices can be attched to the register even though the service itself cannot be searched by a company. The Global Legal Entity Identifier Foundation \cite{gleif} publishes an exact and openly licensed bridge between a company and its LEI.


The second group identifies a company only by the name it trades under, so its records can be attached only through entity resolution, which is probabilistic judgement rather than a lookup. The Intellectual Property Office trademark register \cite{ukipo_tm} records an owner name and a partial postcode but no registration number, so a trademark can be attributed only on the strenght of the name and a coarse location. UK Research and Innovation's Gateway to Research \cite{ukri_gtr} describes publicly funded reasearch projects and reserves a registration number field that is not populated in pratice. Adzuna \cite{adzuna_api} publishes employer names and locations but cannot be queried bu employer, only by keyword and location. The news interfaces, including the Guardian Open Platform \cite{guardian_api}, NewsAPI, GNews and GDELT, return free text in which companies are named as a jounalist would name them, usually without a legal suffix and often without any location at all. Listed company financials, which are keyed on stock tickers rahter than on either a company number or a name, are recored here as an approach we tried and set aside, and the reasons are given with the other coverage results in \S\ref{sec:sources-eval}

So to solve this we created an entity called a spine: a central table containing one record per company with its standardised name, previous name, postcode and any external identifiers that could be linked back to it. This ensures that we make all matches against one consistent company list, stored only once with a confidence score that can be reused by future users. We describe the process fo creating this entity spine in \S\ref{sec:unstructured-method}

\subsection{The initial June 2026 snapshot}
Once we obtained the June snapshot comprising the $\sim5$ mln companies, we decided to narrow it down for initial testing. We utilised the official SIC codes list \cite{companieshouse_sic}, scraping the full 731 codes. From this, we mapped 325 of them onto our 3 target sectors: Technology, Legal \& Professional, Manufacturing and Fast growth \& emerging. See \ref{appx:sic-specific-codes} for more details on how the mapping logic works. This left us with 1,372,321 companies\footnote{The join key we used here and for every table in this report is stated once here and never restated: \texttt{CompanyNumber}, zero-padded to eight digits.}

Here it is worth noting that the register maintained by CH records a company's status (active, insolvent, etc.), but does not carry any information on when or why it switched to a particular status. For this reason, we later incorporate data from the \textit{Gazette}, the UK's official public record, which publishes formal notices relating to corporate insolvency. These notices cover a range of insolvency events, including administrations, receiverships, voluntary liquidations, winding-up petitions and winding-up orders, and can contain event-specific information such as the date on which a petition was filed and the date on which a winding-up order was made \cite{gazette_insolvency}. This makes The Gazette particularly valuable for reconstructing the sequence of events preceding and surrounding insolvency, rather than relying only on a company's observed status in the CH register. This distinction is important as it explains why every target in \S\ref{sec:targets} is defined as an event occurring within a forward window rather than as a state observed at a particular point in time.

\subsection{Acquiring the historical data}
We quiclky incurred a limitation as the single snapshot described a company today, but did not express change, which is essential to encapsulate the modelling signal. More fundamentally, a single snapshot contains limitations on what to predict as it has no time-linked target event. For this reason, we successfully attempted to create a time series dataset, solving both problems at once and turning the target label into a future value of a column we already hold.

To do so, we dug deeper into the CH website and found older monthly .zips stored on the server, reachable by direct URL. These file names are not reliably dated to the first of the month, so rather than guessing, we probed days 1 to 15 of each candidate month once and recorded the dates that resolved. This gave us a manifest of 33 snapshots from October 2023 to July 2026. We keep all 33 zips, roughly 15 GB, permanently: the server ages older months off, at which point our copy is the only one, and re-deriving the panel with a different column set then costs no downloads at all.

While doing this operation, we encountered three methodological considerations that changed our design moving forward:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The obvious approach would have been to replay the SIC filter on every month independently. That is wrong, because a company can be re-coded out of our target sectors without dying, and under a per-month filter that company simply disappears from the panel, which is indistinguishable from a dissolution. So we built the panel on two passes: pass one collects every \texttt{CompanyNumber} matching our sectors in any month, and pass two emits a row for every member of that universe present in a given month, whatever its status or current sector. Absence from the panel now means the firm is gone from the register. The \texttt{recoded\_out\_of\_sector} column records how often this mattered.
    \item Secondly. we decided to keeping every status. Filtering monthly to "Active", as we did in the first filtering logic, would delete every failure event and produce a panel in which no company ever fails, leaving a credit-risk model with nothing to learn from. Insolvent companies are not prospects, but they are precisely the labels we need. They stay in the panel, and we apply \texttt{is\_active} at scoring time instead, the signal \S\ref{sec:targets} keys off.
    \item A third, smaller decision protects point-in-time correctness at build time: the static feature computation is passed the real snapshot date rather than the date whenever the code happens to run.
\end{enumerate}

This results in a panel holding 49,556,152 rows over roughly 2 mln companies, stored long rather than wide: one row per company per month, so a company's history is read down the rows rather than across the columns. We saved the bulk dataset as 33 parquet files keyed on the month start, with the true extract date carried alongside as \texttt{source\_date}.

\subsection{How the distress and growth labels were defined}
The original plan defined the distress label as "Active at $t$ and non-Active by $t+H$", but this ran at roughly 4\%, of which about 90\% was voluntary strike-off rather than a credit event. We therefore narrowed the definition to genuine insolvency by explicitly listing the 10 relevant statuses, which dropped the rate to about 0.34\%. We then modelled the two outcomes separately as Credit Risk Exposure and Voluntary Exit.

For Voluntary Exit, measuring whether a company still sits in proposal to strike off at exactly $t+6$ gives 3.4-3.8\%, whereas measuring whether a proposal appeared at any month in the window gives 6.7-8.5\%. This gap is represented by those proposals raised and then withdrawn, about half of the proposals at every origin. We keep the event definition because a withdrawn proposal is still an event we think a RM wants to know about, as there is still room for intervention. The honest cost of this is that Voluntary Exit means a strike-off process started, but not that the company died; we flag this here for interpretation.

Finally, the \texttt{segment} Field mixes real-size tiers with filing states, such as dormant and no filings. So a naive segment change test runs at about 13\% over 12 months, and it is mostly filing churn. We therefore engineered the Growth Signal to rank only the four genuine tiers (Micro, Small, Medium and Large), excluding companies with no rank rather than assigning them one.

\subsection{Quarterly Origins \& Train/Test Split Design Choices}
\label{sec:train-test-split-logic}
We decided to keep the label origins quarterly rather than creating a training example for every month for a more distributed result. However, this causes the six- and twelve-month targets to overlap by factors of two (6/3) and four (12/3) respectively. This is particularly dangerous for leakage, as the same company can appear multiple times in training and validation if the split is performed randomly. For this reason, we applied a grouped cross-validation in \S\ref{sec:pipeline}, splitting between training and validation by keeping the same company's observations across one or the other. Moreover, label windows are expressed as calendar range frames rather than positional offsets, for the same reason the delta features are: a positional frame would silently reach an extra month past the June 2025 hole. Finally, where a window contains no observed month at all, the label is NULL and never zero, to separate unknown from unhappened.

The split we created follows this logic: train on early origins, test on late ones, with an embargo of at least $H$ months between them so that training labels do not extend into the test period. The quarterly grid pays off here. Lending has a three-month horizon and quarterly origins, so consecutive origins are already exactly $H$ apart and it loses nothing to the embargo, which is why it gets 5 training origins where the six-month targets get 3. The twelve-month growth label pays the full price, losing 3 origins. 

To make things more robust, we implemented a guard, \texttt{FIRST\_FULL\_ORIGIN} (October 2024), that marks the first month when every feature has enough historical data to be properly formed. However, if enforcing that rule would leave us with fewer than 3 training origins, we relax it rather than train on an unusably small sample. For the twelve-month target, this fallback always fires, which means that that target has less ideal training history than the other targets and the pipeline has to relax the guard to get enough training origins. This turns out to matter and is treated as a limitation in \S\ref{sec:limits} rather than as a design choice here.

\subsection{Negative downsampling}
We faced a significant problem due to the scarcity of targets. At a base rate near $3:1000$, most of the 1.4 mln rows per origin are negatives that teach a model nothing, and carrying them costs real computational time. For this reason, we kept every positive but downsampled the negative observations. Sampling was done per origin month so the origin mix is preserved for the out-of-time split described in \S\ref{sec:train-test-split-logic}, and negatives are selected by a hash of the company number rather than a random draw, which makes the sample reproducible without threading a seed through the query engine and, more importantly, keeps the choice consistent for a company across origins. We verified that the scaled sampled positive rate lands at 1:10 for all four targets, confirming it was correctly downsampled to our desired rate. We recorded the fraction of negative observations that we retained under the \textit{keep rate}, which we carried as a column on every row. This is crucial as downsampling negatives at rate $r$ multiplies the odds by $1/r$ and inflates every predicted probability by roughly thirtyfold. While the probability ranking survives that, being a monotone transform, the numbers do not, and a statement that a company has a x\% chance of insolvency is false until we correct it by:
$\text{odds}_{\text{true}} = r \cdot \text{odds}_{\text{sampled}}$.

\subsection{Verification}
Finally, we performed three checks to determine if our design choiches where correct. First, we tested leakage: we measured the correlation of every feature with every label (Figure~\ref{fig:feature-label-corr}), and the strongest is only 0.37, with an economically sensible explanation, where larger companies, those already carrying debt, and those that recently took on charges are the most likely to borrow again. No near-unit correlation undermines the result. Second, we stress-tested the June 2025 hole. On the label side, the April 2025 origin has a three-month window over May, June, and July, and it correctly builds a normal label from May and July rather than reaching into August or going silently null. Third, we measured if the downsampling produced base-rates that remained stable across the eleven origins. The frequencies remained stable and well within the base-rate ranges showed in Table~\ref{tab:targets}, meaning that the model scores are more likely to come from the model genuinely predicting the event, rather than test months just happening to have way more (or fewer) events than the training months. Third, we tested base rate stability. All four rates are flat across T11 origins and fall inside the plausibility ranges written down before measuring, which is what licenses the out-of-time split. Since we see no drift in the underlying rate, any test set improvement is more credibly the model's predictive ability than an artifact of a different event rate.

In this section we go through the machinery that enabled us to run multiple models and features setups at scale and compare them consistently.

\subsection{The three axes for a run, model families \& evaluation settings}
Firstly, we need to establish what the three axes are that constitute a run: which matrix is read (i.e. the precomputead feature table), which feature list (i.e. which columns from the matrix the model is told to use) and which model gets fitted. In our previous design, each was a separate keyword argument threaded through six functions, which meant they had to agree else the run would have silently done the wrong thing. To fix this crippled design, we tied the three axes into one frozen \texttt{RunConfig}, which makes the illegal combinations unpresentable and makes a run reproducible from a single value. We applied these changes after a supervisor review on the 27th July 2026, after which we turned the two hard-coded fitting functions called by name inside the training loop into a registry dictionary in which each entry knows how to build a complete model architecture, whether it requires the dense preprocessing path (i.e. special preprocessing used for dense models), which SHAP explainer applies to it, and what its hyperparameter grid is. The training loop iterates the registry. This, allowed us to later add a new model family (the NN) by simply adding one dictionary entry.

We use the three model families according to this rationale:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gradient-boosted trees} \cite{ke2017lightgbm} is the model we actually deploy and use in the final dashboard.
    \item \textbf{Regularised logistic regression} is the interpretable floor, meaning that if the boosting cannot clearly beat a linear model on the same features, then it is not earning its complexity. Moreover, its signed coefficients serve as an independent check on the SHAP directions, since a disagreement means one of the two its wrong.
    \item The \textbf{multi-layer perceptron} occupies the complex end of the interpretability trade-off.
\end{itemize}

Both dense families share a single preprocessing block of imputation, scaling and one-hot encoding\footnote{From scikit documentation: IMPUTATION replaces missing feature values with estimated values based on the observed data; SCALING transforms numerical features to a comparable scale, commonly by centring them at zero and dividing by their standard deviation; and ONE-HOT encoding represents a categorical variable as a set of binary indicator variables, one for each category. These transformations are standard preprocessing steps for machine-learning estimators. \cite{scikit_learn_preprocessing}}. This is deliberate, because if the network and the linear floor saw differently prepared features, the comparison between them would be about the preprocessing rather than about the model.

During imputation, missingness is flagged rather than filled, via an imputer configured to add an indicator column. A NULL delta means "cannot say", and that is information the boosted model expresses natively, and the dense models cannot express at all without the indicator. The behaviour of that same imputer on a column with no observed value in training turns out to matter a great deal, and is taken up in \S\ref{sec:limits}.



Additionally, we implemented three smaller decisions to protect the evaluation. First, early stopping validates on a company-grouped slice of the training rows and never on the test set, since using the test set to make a training decision would compromise the independence of the final estimate; Scikit-learn offers no way to supply a group slice \cite{scikit-learn_early_stopping_gradient_boosting}, so the network may train somewhat longer than ideal, but we ensured that the out-of-time test set stayed fully independent. Second, the dense models are row capped at 500,000 and 300,000 rows respectively, because backpropagation over a dense one-hot matrix is the slowest operation in the module, and we used it more for a comparison rather than looking to obtain the best neural network result. Third, the evaluation matrices are rebuilt with no downsampling, because P@$N$ does not survive negative downsampling.

To tune, we could not rely on the default $k$-fold split, because that would reintroduce inside the search exactly the two leaks the primary split exists to prevent. We therefore built a splitter that applies the origin and embargo rule of \S\ref{sec:train-test-split-logic} recursively inside the training origins, never touching the test origins. The cost is that the number of folds becomes a maximum rather than a guarantee. The cost is that the number of folds becomes a maximum rather than a guarantee, and that cost is larger than expected:

Growth cannot be tuned at all. A twelve-month embargo across three training origins leaves no validation origin with anything legal to train on, so the splitter returns nothing and the search refuses rather than returning a degenerate answer. A standard $k$-fold would have manufactured five folds for a target that holds no independent evidence whatsoever. Insolvency and voluntary exit return one fold each, where lending returns three. This is the splitter's honesty priced out: on one target of four, the price is total. We measured what adopting the search winner would do rather than assuming it. On lending, the target with the most folds, refitting LightGBM at the winning configuration and pairing it against the shipped default over 2,782,591 held-out rows moves ROC-AUC by $-0.0008$, with a paired interval of $[-0.0013, -0.0003]$ that excludes zero on the negative side. P@$500$ moves $+0.018$ with a lower bound of exactly $+0.000$, which \S\ref{sec:errors} disqualifies on its own rule. Adopting the tuned parameters would have made the shipped model very slightly worse. The reason is worth stating, because it is a property of the search rather than of the model. RandomizedSearchCV cannot be handed an evaluation set, so every configuration is scored at a fixed 1,500 boosting rounds. At 1,500 rounds a slow, narrow configuration is still improving while a fast, wide one overfitted long ago, so what the search ranks is how gracefully a configuration tolerates being run far past its useful length. The production path never goes there: under company-grouped early stopping the shipped default stops at 143 rounds and the tuned winner at 262. This is why the search picks the same corner of the grid on all three tunable targets, 31 leaves at learning rate 0.03 with a minimum of 100 child samples, and ranks the shipped default tenth of twelve every time. That consistency looks like a strong signal and is an artefact of the fixed round count. The search and the deployed model are not optimising the same object. The parameters were therefore never fed back into a fit. Every recorded run sits at the same fixed boosting parameters, and each tuning payload records this as applied: false.

The pipeline also verifies the recalibration of the downsampling. As mentioned, downsampling negatives at rate $r$ multiplies the odds by $1/r$, so we implement a correction: one line applied to every predicted probability, using the keep rate that \S\ref{sec:targets} deliberately carried through on every row. The check is that the mean recalibrated prediction lands on the true base rate of the test population, which is the cheapest available test that the correction is the right way round and would be an easy thing to get backwards without noticing. 

Finally, we also need to explain the models. We programmed the pipeline so that it automatically chooses an appropriate explanation method for each one:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The dispatcher routes the boosted model (LightGBM) to \texttt{TreeExplainer}, which is exact, but it does so on a stratified subsample of 100,000 test rows; stratification matters here, since a plain draw at a base rate of 0.33\% holds around 330 positives and the resulting summary plot would describe the negatives almost exclusively.
    \item Logistic regression is read through its coefficients rather than explained, for the reason given in \S\ref{sec:modelling-backgroud}.
    \item The neural network is much harder to explain, so we use the more general KernelExplainer, but only on 20 observations because it is computationally expensive. The measured costs are in \S\ref{sec:results}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Recording a run}
Every run owns a directory holding a manifest (.json), its metrics (.json), and one SHAP importance table (.csv) per target, with a regenerated index across all runs. The manifest is the part that matters: it records the matrix directory, the evaluation directory, the contract and lender sources, the exact feature list together with a short hash of it, the categoricals, the targets, the model set, the registry as it stood, the boosting parameters, the git commit and the timestamp. A run is reproducible from its manifest without reading any code, and two runs are compared by diffing two manifests rather than by remembering what was done. We also deliberately programmed the writer so that it refuses to overwrite an existing tag: the baseline run is the fixed reference the whole A/B design depends on, and under the previous flat layout re-running a single cell would have silently destroyed it. We also have a index containing all our runs, which is regenerated from the directories on every write, so the comparison table cannot drift from what is actually on disk. 
Execution is staged through a script rather than a Jupyter notebook, and the reason is a technical limitation rather than an architectural one: the machine kept rebooting under long jobs. A run therefore had to be resumable and had to survive losing its terminal. The same principle was used for the 25-hour Charges data harvest of \S\ref{sec:charges} recoverable from its own output rather than from a side ledger. 
Finally, we found a WSL2-specific performance problem: asking LightGBM to use all 22 logical cores caused severe thread contention. Restricting it to eight cores made the same computation dramatically faster. Fifty boosting rounds on the 165,000-row insolvency matrix took 98 seconds with unlimited threads and 0.9 seconds with eight, for an identical result.

The module map is given in Table~\ref{tab:modules} and the full run registry in Appendix~\ref{appx:runs}.

\subsection{Baseline}
The \texttt{baseline} run is the 41-feature control matrix fitted with the boosted and linear families only, recorded before the registry rewrite and before the network existed. It is retained rather than superseded because it is the fixed reference against which the added value of the Charges API harvest is measured, and destroying it would have destroyed that measurement rather than merely inconveniencing us.

It is not, however, a like-for-like row in the results table, and Chapter 4 says so wherever it appears. It holds eight rows rather than twelve because it predates the third model family, and
it predates the bootstrap, so it carries point estimates with no intervals attached. 

After rewriting the pipeline, we ran the same baseline configuration through the new code. The three reported metrics were exactly the same as before: ROC-AUC 0.761520, PR-AUC 0.016923 and P@$100$ 19.0\%. This confirms that the software rewrite changed the structure of the code without changing the underlying modelling result.

\section{How we collected unstructured data}
\label{sec:unstructured-method}
The sources introduced in \ref{sec:public-sources} were collected between the begining of June and the middle of August 2026 and attached to the register through a single matching procedure. The procedure is the same for every source; what differs is the identifier the source publishes, and that difference alone accounts for most of the variation in coverage reported in \S\ref{sec:sources-eval}. The results themselves are discussed in \S\ref{sec:unstructured}.

\subsection{Matching, and the confidence ladder}
\label{sec:matching-ladder}

Where a source publishes a registration number, its records join to the register directly once both values are formatted to eight characters with any letter prefix preserved, and no futher work is required. Where a source publishes only a name, the record is passed through a sequence of progressively weaker checks and teh first that succeeds is accepted: the normalised company name together with a full postcode, provided that pair identifies exactly one company; the normalised name together with a postcode area, used where the source publishes only the outward code; and the normalised name together with the registered town.

If a key identifies more than one company the match is rejected rather than guessed, and records carrying no location at all are discarded rather than matched on the name alone. These choices reduce coverage deliberately in order to raise precision. An early contracts run without a location check credited an Oxford company named "Amazon Ltd" with Amazon Web Services awards and an "Ace Tax Ltd" with taxi work , and for a read who ahs to act on the output a wrong company is more damaging than a missing one. Every accepted match therefore carries a confidence score between 0.80 and 1.00 according to the rung that produced it, and anything below 1.00 is marked as a name match wherever it is shown.

\subsection{The Five Sources}

Public contracts were collected from Contracts Finder and Find a Tender over a rolling window and cached on first retrieval. Where an award notice carries a supplier identifier the record joins on the company number; where it does not, the supplier name is used together with the postcode recovered from the supplier address.

Property was taken from the Land Registry Commerical ownership extract , read in blocks to stay within memory and expanded so that each of the proprietors recorded against a title becomes a row of its own. Every match here is exact, because the file carries a registration number for each corporate owner, which makes this the cleanest of the five. Title with no properietor data cannot be placed on a timeline and are retaiend separately rather than discarded.

Trade marks were collected from the Intellectual Property Office open data release, a pipe separated file in UTF-16 whose encoding had to be establised before it would parse at all. The register carries a owner name and a postcode area but no registration number, so matching rests on the name confirmed by the area, which is the coarsest location check of the five.

Research grants were collected from UK Research and Innovation's Gateway to Research, Paginated and cached page by page so that an interrupted run could resume. The organisation records carry a name a postcode, and although the schema reserves a registration number field it is not populated, so these are matched by name as well.

Job adverts were collected from Adzuna. The interface cannot be queired by employer, only by keyword and location, so the national feed was retreived and the employer names matched back against the register. This is the weakest link of the five, because the feed is dominated by large employers and recruitmet agencies and the only location it publishes is a town.

\subsection{Output shape and reproducibilty}

Every source writes into two files of the same shape, whatever its origin. The first holds on row per dated event, carrying the company number, the date, the event type, a readable description, a value where one exists, a link back to the source record, the confidence score and the rung of the ladder that produced the match. The second holds one row per company, summarising counts, earliest and latest dates, recent activity and totals. Cleaning and writing are handled by one shared module, so adding a source means producing an event table in the expected shape and nothing further.

That common shape is what allowed these sources to be attched to the delivered system described in \S\ref{sec:delivery} alongside the register itself, and it is the reason a single company profile can place a contract award, a property title and a statutory notice on one timeline with the strength of each match visible beside it. Absense is recorded as zero rather than left empty , because a company that was searched and found to have no record is a different statement from a company that was never searched. Every response is cached on first retreival, so a run can be produced without the network and a demonstation does not depend on a third party being available at the time.

\section{Dashboard}
The dashboard brings the project's separate outputs into one place where a company can be thoroughly investigated. The dashboard is therefore designed as the final layer connecting these pieces, rather than replacing the underlying analysis.

The population is the July 2026 CH universe of 1,531,094 companies. Six sources are connected to this spine through company number: Gazette notices, contracts, property titles, trade marks, grants and news. The division between them is deliberate. \section{Engineering practice}

A constant risk in a project like this is that the code used to explore an idea and the code used to finally train the model can slowly drift apart. So that chart in a notebook can no longer be reproduced by the pipeline. Three practical steps was taken to prevent this.

\paragraph{One shared definition of every feature}
Feature or label logic was never re-written in each notebook. It was defined once, in a shared Python package \texttt{(src/features)} and \texttt{(src/models)} and imported by every notebook rather than copied. The panel and its deltas, the contract and lender features, the targets and training pipeline were all defined there and called from the exploratory notebooks. This guarantees that the matrix used for modelling is exactly the same one we explored.

It also makes the logic testtable, the leakage and consistency tests run against the same functions that build the features, rather than against a separate notebook version that might quietly diverge. The modelling pipeline itself was built on scikit-learn \cite{sklearn_docs}, with a small registry so that adding a new model was a small change rather than edit threaded through several functions.

\paragraph{A branch per workstream}
Since five people worked in paralled, version control was organised as one feature branch per workstream rather than a single shared line. Each strand namely filtering, attrition, the structured panel and modelling, the unstructured lab and harmonisation owned its own branch so work processed without people overwriting one another, and the history recorded who did what and when.

\paragraph{Runs that could be re-checked}
Every modelling run writes down exactly how it has been produced. Alongside its metrics, each run wrote its full configuration, which feature matrix was used, the exact ordered list of features and the short \texttt{feature\_hash} , the model settings, the code version and a timestamp into small record on disk and refused to overwrite an existing one. Two things followed. A run could be reproduced from its record without reading any code and two runs could be compared by simply looking at the difference between their records. Any changes in the results is always traceable to change in inputs, not to an untracked edit.

\subsection{Evaluation design}
\label{sec:evaldesign}
\note{State the rules before the numbers, so no result later looks chosen after
the fact. Why ranking rather than classification. Why ROC-AUC and P@$N$
together, and what each one actually answers: ROC-AUC is a global ranking score
that survives the negative downsampling; P@$N$ is what a relationship
manager experiences. Why per-origin rather than pooled, which \S\ref{sec:errors}
then justifies with a measurement. What the bootstrap intervals resample.}

Before we delve into the resulting number we obtained we need to explain the metrics we picked and why we chose those. As established in \S\ref{sec:modelling-backgroud}, the final task of our deliverable is ranking rather than classification, so the evaluation is one of ordering. For this reason, two metrics are reported for every model on every target, because they answer different questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item ROC-AUC measures how well the model ranks positive cases above negative cases across the whole population. It has the useful property that it survives the 10:1 negative downsampling of \S\ref{sec:targets} in expectation, since dropping a uniform random subset of negatives does not change pairwise ordering. 
    \item P@$N$ is different. It looks only at the top of the ranking, for example the top 100 or top 500 companies. This is much closer to what a RM actually experiences. However, P@N is strongly affected by downsampling.
\end{itemize}

Every test origin is therefore rebuilt with no downsampling before anything is measured, using
the same matrix builder with the keep rate capped at one.

All operational figures are reported per origin rather than pooled. Section \S\ref{sec:errors} explains why this is necessary.

Every metric carries a 95\% confidence interval obtained by resampling the evaluation rows with replacement, one thousand replicates, percentile method. These confidence intervals should be interpreted carefully. They measure uncertainty caused by which companies happened to be in the evaluation sample. They do not include uncertainty from choosing different training periods or from fitting the model again with a different random seed. They are therefore a lower bound on total uncertainty. When comparing two models evaluated on exactly the same companies at the same origins, we use a paired bootstrap: both models are evaluated on the same bootstrap sample, so the variation that is common to both models cancels out. This gives a more precise estimate of the difference between the models. \S\ref{sec:errors} shows that the unpaired reading is inconclusive on the comparison that matters most, where the paired reading is not.

Finally, six of the eleven recorded runs are directly comparable to one another and carry bootstrap intervals on all twelve cells. The remaining five are retained as diagnostic rather than comparative: three are superseded by deterministic re-recordings that reproduce them exactly, and two are kept as exhibits of the defect described in §\ref{sec:leak}, where an interval on a number we are presenting as broken would serve no purpose. The body therefore tabulates the six comparable runs; all eleven appear in Appendix~\ref{appx:runs}.

    \item Voluntary exit is strongly related to filing behaviour. Here we flag a methodological correction when interpreting SHAP. As mentioned, we validate SHAP directions against the signed coefficients of the linear model, on the principle that where the two disagree one of them is wrong. Hence, the average signed SHAP value is not a measure of direction. A feature can have different effects at different values. Therfore, direction is measured using the Spearman correlation between the feature value and its own SHAP value. This does not assume that the relationship is linear.

Most features give similar directions in the SHAP analysis and the logistic model. The main disagreements occur for insolvency. This is not necessarily a problem because logistic coefficients measure a feature's effect while holding the other variables constant, while SHAP shows the feature's relationship with predictions. Hence, when features are strongly correlated, a partial effect can honestly flip sign against the marginal relationship SHAP displays.

\subsection{Is the target balanced, and what does that do to the metrics?}
The four targets we are aiming to predict have very different base rates. They range from 0.26\% for lending to about 8\% for voluntary exit. This matters because the same predicted metric can mean very different things based on the target. For instance, if a model predicts "no" for every company on the lending task, it would achieve 99.74\% accuracy. On the surface, that sounds excellent, but the model would be misleading given that its predicting accuracy on a very low base rate. The same problem applies to recall, precision and F1 performance metrics. Instead, we use ranking metrics such as ROC-AUC and P@$N$. We also report a column \texttt{lift}, defined as P@$N$ divided by the target's base rate. This is the only fair way to compare a result on a target that fires 1:100 with one that fires 1:12.

This imbalance also affects how much downsampling we need:
\begin{itemize}
    \item For insolvency, the dataset is reduced by about 27 times, from 13.8 mln rows to 520,000, without removing any positive cases.
    \item Lending is reduced by about 33 times.
    \item Voluntary exit is different: the dataset only falls from 13.8 mln to 11.9 mln rows, with a keep rate of 0.85. This happens because voluntary exit has a much higher base rate of about 7.8\%, so there is little need to remove additional negative observations.
\end{itemize}

The number of positive cases also determines how precise the confidence intervals can be:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Voluntary exit has about 215,000 positive cases, so it has the narrowest intervals, around ±0.001.
    \item Lending has only about 7,500 positive cases, so its intervals are much wider, around ±0.0045.
\end{itemize}

We must also interpret with caution the results of voluntary exit. Its apparently strong P@$N$ is partly caused by many companies receiving exactly the same score. Therefore, P@$N$ is partly measuring which companies were selected from a large tied group, rather than measuring a precise ranking, for reasons measured in \S\ref{sec:errors}, and it should not be read to three digits.


\subsection{A leakage defect, found and fixed}
\label{sec:leak}
\note{The strongest methodological section in the report, and per the 8 August
decision it stays a section here rather than becoming its own chapter. Tell it
in four acts. (i) The lender run returns P@$100$ = 1.000 on lending
against a 0.271\% base rate, while ROC-AUC barely moves; explain why that
combination is a leakage signature rather than a win, because a genuinely better
feature set lifts the global ranking and the top of the list together. (ii) The
cause: the as-of gate admitted a charge on \texttt{created\_on}, but 94.7\% of
charges are delivered to CH after they are created, so the model
could see a charge the register had not yet recorded, and the register is what
defines the label. (iii) The obvious fix was not enough: gating on
\texttt{delivered\_on} removed about half of it. (iv) Calibrating properly showed
that 18 of the 21 days of the interim margin were the \emph{satisfaction} clock,
not a visibility lag, so one gate had been widened to pay for a different gate
being wrong. Include the retraction: the extract-drift hypothesis was
satisfying, and it was worth 0.11 days of 21, and it ran the opposite way to
prediction. Also state that the original verification test inherited the bug
rather than catching it, because it replayed the harvest against the same wrong
clock, and that a single-month version of the rewritten test would still have
passed. Figures: the gate progression on lending P@500, the calibration ladder,
the lag-error plots.}

\subsubsection{The symptom}
Since we already started with a pretty solid model, including many deltas, the original comparison was designed to test if the 25 hours of Charges API harvest improved prediction. 

For three of the four targets, the answer appeared to be no. However, the lending result looked extremely strong: P@$100$ of 1.000 against a base rate of 0.271\%, and
P@$500$ of 0.992, a lift of 366 times, while ROC-AUC moved only from 0.8767 to 0.8951.

These two things cannot co-exist, and it's the reason why \S\ref{sec:evaldesign} reports both metrics. If the model had generally become much better at ranking companies, both ROC-AUC and P@$N$ should have improved substantially. Instead, P@$N$ became almost perfect, while ROC-AUC changed only slightly. 

The explanation is that ROC-AUC looks at th,e ranking across about 2.8 mln evaluation rows, while P@$N$ looks only at the very top of the list. Hence, if the model receives information about the outcome for only a small group of companies, that can make the top of the list look almost perfect, while having very little effect on the global ranking. The gap between a global metric and a local one is the entire tell, and had we tracked only AUC we would have shipped this.

Before explaining this bizarreness with leakage, we ruled out several simpler explanations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The test rows and the positive counts were identical between the runs, so the comparison was fair.
    \item The lender features existed for 100\% of the rows because we designed for companies without charges to be assigned assigned 0 rather than a NULL. Hence, this rules out any selection effect.
    \item Finally, the best individual feature had a P@$500$ of only 0.164, while the full model reached 0.992. This meant that no individual column was driving the label. On the contrary, the model was combining several features, which demanded further analysis rather than isolating one single problematic feature.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{The cause}
Every charge in the CH API carries: \texttt{created\_on}, the date the charge was made; \texttt{delivered\_on}, the date it reached the registrar and became visible to anyone looking.

The original as-of join used the first one and this created the leakage.

Imagine that a charge was created on 20 January, but delivered on 28 January. The original system could include this charge in the feature row for 1 January, even though the charge was not yet visible at that time. 

The lending label, however, was calculated from the bulk register. That register could not contain the charge before it had been delivered. The feature, therefore, contained information that the label could only know later. 

The model was not actually predicting future lending. Instead, it was partly reading information about something that had already happened.

We also measured this disruption, making the size of the problem even clearer. 

For companies that went on to borrow within three months, the API's charge count was already 0.29 charges higher than the register's count. In 19.2\% of these cases, the API had already reached the future post-borrowing value. 

Across the whole population that same gap is about 0.05, so the discrepancy is six times larger precisely where it does damage.

If we then consider the top 500 companies ranked by the model, 46.6\% had a charge that was counted before it was observable. Across the whole population, this happened for only 0.01\% of companies. This was the strongest evidence of leakage.

\subsubsection{The obvious fix we attempted}

The first obvious solution we attempted to fix the problem was to use \texttt{delivered\_on} instead of \texttt{created\_on}. This reduced the problem substantially, moving the mean gap from 0.29 to 0.16 and the share of companies already at the future value from 19.2\% to 9.7\%, but the problem was not gone altogether. After rerunning the model, lending P@$500$ was still 0.742, compared with 0.282 for the control model.

After digging deeper into how the charge API works, we discovered that delivery is not the same as registration: after a charge is delivered, the registrar still needs time to process it before it appears in the register used to compute the label. To estimate that delay, we ran a sweep: increasing a margin day by day until the API's charge count agreed on average with the register's count. Three choices went into how we ran it, each a sensible-looking default at the time. We swept over only seven monthly origins rather than all of them, to limit the cost of repeatedly rebuilding and comparing the panel. We assumed each register extract was cut on the 1st of the month, which is what the monthly bulk file is nominally dated and is true the large majority of the time. And we measured the gap only on companies that go on to borrow (the subset where the distortion is worst) on the instinct of calibrating where the problem actually shows up. Comparing the two counts this way, the margin that made them agree on average came out at 21 days, and that is the figure we fitted.

\subsubsection{The calibration}
The 21-day value initially looked reasonable, but we decomposed it. Walking from the old estimate to the new one, changing one thing at a time with the same estimator throughout, we created an audit trail that helped us in our calibration. The aim was to figure out how many days of "margin" to add so that the API's view of a company's charges lined up with the register's view. We did so by sweeping the margin until the two counts agreed on average. The 21-day answer came out of one particular way of running that sweep, and this decomposition re-runs it correctly, one change at a time, to see where the 21 came from:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Using all origins rather than seven moves the crossing from 22.1 to 23.7 days
    \item Using the real extract date moves it from 23.7 to 23.6
    \item Pointing the sweep at the right target moves it from 23.6 to 5.3: the register count it had been matching against moves both when a charge is created and when one is satisfied, so calibrating the creation clock against it forced the margin to absorb the satisfaction (i.e. a charge exiting when repaid) clock's error too; matching against a count that reflects creation alone removes that.
    \item and finally, removing the conditioning on companies that go on to borrow moves it from 5.3 to 1.8.
\end{itemize}

The key result is that 18 of the 21 days came from the satisfaction clock. 

 There was also independent evidence of the leakage inside the model itself. In fact, under the leaking \texttt{created\_on} gate, \texttt{n\_of\_charges\_outstanding} was the most important feature, with a mean absolute SHAP value of 0.414, ranking 1st out of 54 features. After we changed to \texttt{delivered\_on}, it fell to 0.284, ranking 3rd. And after we added the 21-day margin, it fell almost completely to 0.002, ranking 40th. A similar pattern appeared for \texttt{lbg\_share\_of\_outstanding}, whose importance fell from 0.146 to 0.073 to 0.002. 
 
This mattered because it is not derived from the metric we are trying to explain. P@$100$ = 1.000 tells
us the top of the list is wrong. Thanks to the SHAP ranking, we could understand which feature the model was using to obtain that wrong information. Two independent instruments, one mechanism.

Moving on to other results we obtained from the decomposition, we originally thought that most of the 21 days were caused by differences in the monthly extract dates. In reality, we showed this explains only 0.11 days. The extract date usually was the first of the month: 29 of 33 extracts were on the first, with an average drift of 0.39 days. The correction actually goes in the opposite direction from what we expected because an extract taken after the first includes more charges, not fewer. So extract drift is real, but it is not the main explanation.

The deeper issue is that the register is only observed once a month. If a charge is delivered on the tenth, it cannot appear in the observed register until the next monthly extract, regardless of how quickly the registrar processes it. Therefore, matching the average API count to the monthly register can incorrectly interpret waiting time as processing time.

\subsubsection{The final solution}
Instead of a mean-matching sweep, we calibrated on first appearance. For each charge, we find the first monthly extract in which the register shows that charge. This gives\footnote{The confidence intervals collapse to a single point because the estimated value is stable, not because the underlying criterion is beyond debate.}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item creation lag: 4 days (95\% CI [4, 4], (n=51,669))
    \item satisfaction lag: 1 day (95\% CI [1, 1], (n=47,480))
\end{itemize}

This was the best overall solution.

Moreover, the old 21-day gate carried a large practical cost. The margin exists to prevent leakage, but pushing it too far causes the opposite problem: charges that the register already contained, and that the model was entitled to see, get withheld from the feature row anyway, leaving the model to work from an outdated picture. This is staleness, and at 21 days it was severe. The gate bought only 0.60 percentage points of reduced leakage while paying 43 percentage points of staleness for it, so that more than half of all charges were hidden for a full month after they had genuinely become visible. That is why the 21-day model scored below the plain control: it was no longer peeking at future charges, but it was now blind to past ones it should have used.

But then, why did the original test never caught it?
The original verification test truncated the charge history at a cut-off date and checked whether the rebuilt panel matched the original panel. However, both the truncation and the panel used \texttt{created\_on}. Same clock on both sides, so the test could never fail. In other words, the test was effectively asking if the panel included charges created after the cut-off date. By construction, the answer was no. Instead, we were trying to verify if the panel included charges that were already created but were not yet observable at the cut-off date.

The rewritten test keeps the reference clock and the visibility clock separate. It also checks that rebuilding the panel from the full history reproduces the stored panel. Any difference in the blind replay can therefore be attributed to the truncated history rather than to the test itself.

There is one more important lesson.

Even the rewritten test would have passed if it had been run on only one month. Hence, we tested it on:
\begin{itemize}
    \item January 2025, with no extract drift
    \item February 2024, with the largest drift of six days
\end{itemize}

In February 2024, the six-day extract drift happened to compensate for the missing satisfaction delay. The leakage therefore remained hidden.

From this we learnt that a blind-replay test (i.e. taking the full history, cutting it off at some date, rebuilding what the model should have been able to see at that date, and checking it matches the stored data) is successful contingent on having the same date field for when you cut and when you check against and the range of months you run it on.

The final table and figures document the four gates and their sensitivity range. The main lending P@$500$ sequence is:

0.282 → 0.992 → 0.742 → 0.310 → 0.332

with a sensitivity band from 0.294 to 0.406.

\subsection{Does counterparty identity help? (N4)}

are throwing away on purpose.}

Once again, the whole point of the test was to see if adding features from the Charger API would increase model accuracy against the 41-feature control model. Across the four targets and the three model families, here we present the most conservative result, applying the solution to the leakage explained in \S\ref{sec:leak}.

The strongest evidence comes from the pattern across the gate settings rather than from any one number. As we showed in previous section, the apparent improvement increases monotonically as the visibility gate becomes more permissive. This happens in all three model families and in all nine relevant cells. This is important, If we observed only one model improving as the gate loosened, that would be a model-specific improvement. However, since all three families show the same pattern, it is much more likely that the pattern comes from the data itself.

The reason becomes clear when comparing lending with insolvency. The lending label is calculated from the same charge register controlled by the visibility gate. If the gate is loosened, the model gets earlier access to information that directly contributes to its own label. That creates leakage. On the other hand, the insolvency label comes from a completely different register. The gate affects the features but has no path to the insolvency label.

Therefore, insolvency acts as a control experiment. If the lender features were simply useful information, both lending and insolvency should improve in a similar way regardless of the gate. If the gate is leaking the lending label, only lending should respond strongly to the gate. That is what we observe.

For lending, P@$500$ is 0.294, 0.332 and 0.406 across the three gate settings against a control of 0.282, with the two looser gates clear of zero on the paired interval. For insolvency, there is no similar pattern. The linear model stays around +0.0023 at all three settings. The insolvency MLP does increase across the ladder, but the change is negative and much smaller than its seed variation. It therefore does not provide useful evidence. Removing those MLP rows makes the main argument stronger because the monotonic pattern remains in the two model families whose results are reproducible enough to interpret.

\subsubsection{Verdict by model family: LightGBM}

The boosted model gains essentially nothing from the thirteen additional lender features.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Across the four targets, its largest absolute ROC-AUC change is only 0.0007.
    \item For voluntary exit and growth, the paired intervals actually show small negative changes of -0.0002 and -0.0005.
\end{itemize}

This means that the result is not simply that we did not find evidence of an improvement. More importantly, the evidence shows that the boosted model does not benefit from these additional features and may be very slightly worse.

After 25 hours of data harvesting, the boosted model is already extracting the useful information from the mortgage-charge count features in the original feature set.

The neural network is not interpreted because its seed instability is too large.

\subsubsection{Ineffectiveness of some lenders metrics}
There is also an internal model check supporting the conclusion. In the \texttt{lender\_fixed} LightGBM model, \texttt{is\_lbg\_client} has a mean absolute SHAP value of exactly 0.000000 on all four targets. It ranks between 51st and 53rd out of 54 features. This means that LightGBM never used this feature in a split across its 1,500 trees.

Before interpreting that as evidence, we checked whether the model was actually capable of using the feature: with \texttt{min\_child\_samples} = 200, a binary feature needs at least 200 positive examples to be usable in a split. \texttt{is\_lbg\_client} has between 5,085 and 103,182 positive cases depending on the target, so it easily passes this requirement.

Its zero importance therefore means the model chose not to use the feature.

By contrast, \texttt{competitor\_entered\_12m} also has zero importance, but it does not have enough positive cases for the model to use it reliably on some targets. Its zero importance is therefore a limitation of model capacity, not evidence that the feature is useless.

\texttt{ever\_lbg\_client} and \texttt{lbg\_charge\_satisfied\_6m} are also barely used, with mean absolute SHAP values never above ($3.1\times10^{-4}$).

\subsubsection{Conclusion}
The broader conclusion is more useful than simply saying that the lender features do not help. The additional columns mostly provide a different encoding of information that the base features already contain. Hence, while the boosted model can already extract this information from the raw charge counts, the linear model cannot easily create the interactions needed to do so, so it benefits from the richer encoding.

Therefore, the finding is mainly about encoding rather than new information. Voluntary exit is the exception that keeps the reading honest. There, the linear model is measurably slightly worse with the lender columns, by $-0.0010$ with an interval that excludes zero, which is the same shape as the boosted verdict on voluntary exit and growth. So the claim is not that the richer encoding helps the linear model everywhere. It is that where the base features already carry the charge counts in a form the model can use, the extra columns are at best redundant and can cost a little.
This means the data harvest could still be useful when interpretability is important and a linear model is being used. However, tt is not useful for the boosted model that we actually deploy.

Two caveats are important.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item First, the tightest gate we tested has not been proven completely clean. The lending performance is still increasing at that gate. We therefore know that the remaining lookahead is smaller than at the calibrated gate, but we do not know that it is zero.
    \item Second, the strongest result in the entire project is deliberately being discarded. The loosest gate produces lending P@$500$ of 0.406, which is a 44\% improvement over the control and a lift of roughly 150 times the base rate. However, this is also the gate with the most leakage. Reporting 0.406 as a genuine model improvement would therefore mean reporting a result that is partly caused by the model seeing its own label.
\end{enumerate}

The number is kept in the report so that anyone who finds it elsewhere also sees the explanation for why it is not used.

\subsection{What the model learned: global SHAP analysis}
\label{sec:label-shap-importance}
We created four beeswarm summaries from the control run, one per target at figures~\ref{fig:beeswarm}. This section interprets those graphs and explains what the models actually learned from the data.

There is one important technical point about all SHAP values in this chapter. \texttt{TreeExplainer} for \texttt{LGBMClassifier} gives contributions in log-odds, not probabilities. These contributions add up to the logit of the model's uncalibrated score. Hence, the final score used in production is recalibrated using: $$odds_{true} = odds_{sampled} \times neg\_keep\_rate$$. For example, the top lending company in the July 2026 list has an uncalibrated probability of 0.972 but a final shipped score of 0.500. The ranking does not change because the recalibration is monotonic. Therefore, the SHAP values explain the ranking, while the displayed probability is the recalibrated version of that ranking. The SHAP values should therefore not be added together and expected to reproduce the probability shown on the shortlist.

\subsubsection{Lending}
Lending is mainly a story about company size and existing debt. \texttt{Mortgages.NumMortCharges} is the most important feature, with a mean absolute SHAP value of 0.57. \texttt{segment} follows at 0.46. Companies with no existing charges tend to receive negative SHAP contributions, while companies with many charges tend to receive positive contributions.

This is reasonable for a lending-readiness model, but it also gives a slightly uncomfortable conclusion: the strongest signal for who is likely to borrow next is who has borrowed before. Because of this, a simple top-N ranking will naturally favour larger, highly leveraged companies. An SME team may therefore need to rank companies within size groups rather than across the whole population.

\subsubsection{Voluntary exit}

Voluntary exit is mainly a filing-behaviour story.

The three strongest features are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{months\_since\_last\_confstmt}: 0.38
    \item \texttt{accounts\_stale\_streak\_months}: 0.31
    \item \texttt{days\_to\_next\_accounts\_due}: 0.30
\end{itemize}

These features are interesting because their interpretation changes depending on when they are measured. In a cross-sectional model, they would be leakage. A company that has already stopped filing is already showing the outcome we are trying to predict. But in this project, the features are measured at time (t) and used to predict a strike-off proposal between (t+1) and (t+6). In that setting, the same features become early warning signals.

This is one of the clearest benefits of using a time series: information that would be leakage in a cross-sectional model can become valid predictive information when it is measured before the outcome occurs.

\subsubsection{Insolvency}

Insolvency does not have one dominant feature. \texttt{segment} is the strongest feature, with mean absolute SHAP of 0.73, followed by \texttt{company\_age\_years} at 0.32. After that, importance is spread across age and filing-recency variables. This is consistent with the lower ROC-AUC seen for insolvency. The model is combining many relatively weak signals rather than relying on one powerful predictor. The disadvantage is that there is no simple explanation that can easily be given to a RM for why a particular company appears high in the ranking.

\subsubsection{Growth}
Growth is dominated by \texttt{tier\_rank}, with a mean absolute SHAP value of 0.94, almost twice that of the next feature. This result should be treated carefully. \texttt{tier\_rank} comes from \texttt{Accounts.AccountCategory}, so the model is strongly using the company's current position in the size hierarchy to predict whether it will move to a higher tier. Part of this predictive power is a ceiling effect. A company that is already at the highest tier cannot move to a higher tier. The model may therefore be learning this boundary rather than learning a real mechanism of growth.

This is the weakest of the four SHAP interpretations and should be investigated further.

\subsubsection{Checking the direction of features}

We then compared the direction of the most important features with the signs of the corresponding logistic coefficients. However, there is an important correction to the way direction should be calculated: the average signed SHAP value is not a measure of direction. A feature can have a positive effect for some companies and a negative effect for others. The average can therefore be close to zero or even have the opposite sign from the actual relationship. Instead, we measure direction using the Spearman correlation between the feature's value and its own SHAP value. Using this method, the SHAP and logistic models agree on:
\begin{itemize}
    \item 10/10 top features for growth
    \item 9/10 for voluntary exit
    \item 8/10 for lending
    \item only 3/10 for insolvency
\end{itemize}

The insolvency disagreement is scritically not matching. One possible explanation is that insolvency features have genuinely non-linear relationships, while logistic regression is trying to represent them using straight lines. However, this has not been demonstrated, so it remains a hypothesis.

\subsection{What our error bars are worth}
\label{sec:errors}
\note{Present this as a result, not a footnote, because it is one. Two findings.
(i) We validated our confidence intervals against a second, independent source of
variation, refitting under different seeds, and they held for LightGBM and
logistic and failed for the MLP, where a seed term dominates. In one case two
non-overlapping intervals were reassuring us about a difference whose sign flips
across seeds. State the resulting rule: no MLP difference in this project below
about 0.02 ROC-AUC is interpretable. (ii) The paired bootstrap: comparing two
runs by separate intervals is the wrong test when both are evaluated on the same
companies, and resampling once and differencing tightens the lending interval
about five-fold and moves it from inconclusive to clearly excluding zero.
(iii) Pooled P@$N$ is not an average of its months; it lands outside the
range of the two months it pools in 9 of 12 rows, in both directions, and the
worst row lands 0.108 below the worse of its own months. So per-origin figures
are the honest ones, and it is also what a RM receives, one
month at a time.}

In this section we explore confidence intervals and the uncertainty analysis results, because they changed which conclusions the project could safely make.

\subsubsection{Bootstrap uncertainty versus model instability}

The bootstrap resamples the evaluation companies. It therefore answers how much does the score change depending on which companies happen to be in the test sample. It does not answer what happens if we fit the model again. These are different sources of uncertainty.

When the growth models were fitted using four different random seeds:
\begin{itemize}
    \item LightGBM varied by 0.0013
    \item Logistic regression varied by 0.0021
\end{itemize}

These values are relatively small compared with their bootstrap intervals. For these two models, the row bootstrap therefore captures most of the relevant uncertainty.

The neural network behaves very differently. Its variation is:

\begin{itemize}
    \item 0.018 for the 41-feature model
    \item 0.031 for the corrected 27-feature model
\end{itemize}


These are four to seven times larger than the bootstrap widths. 


In the light of the results, the practical rule we used throughout the chapter is: for the neural network, a ROC-AUC difference below about 0.02 should not be interpreted as meaningful. A difference between two individual fits carries uncertainty of roughly ±0.035.

The growth feature correction showed why this matters. Initially, it appeared that the correction improved the MLP by +0.0075, with non-overlapping bootstrap intervals.

But when both feature sets were fitted using four different seeds, the paired differences were:
\begin{itemize}
    \item +0.0075
    \item +0.0283
    \item -0.0063
    \item -0.0101
\end{itemize}

The sign therefore changed in two of the four runs. The mean difference was only +0.0048, with a standard deviation of 0.0174. Even worse, the recorded 41-feature model was exactly the worst of the four seeds. The apparent improvement was therefore partly caused by which particular seed happened to be used.

The lesson we learnt is that two non-overlapping bootstrap intervals can still give a misleading impression of a difference if the model itself is unstable across seeds. When the same model is fitted twice in the same process with the same inputs, all three model families produce bit-identical predictions. The issue is fit instability across random seeds: the model is reproducible when the seed is fixed, but different seeds produce genuinely different fitted models.

\subsubsection{Numerical reproducibility}
The dense models are exactly reproducible given a threading configuration, and differ between configurations. Refitting one growth cell at one, four, eight and sixteen BLAS threads gives ROC-AUC 0.738222, 0.738209, 0.738222 and 0.738214, a spread of about $1.3\times10^{-5}$, while two fresh processes at the same thread count agree bit for bit. The residual is therefore deterministic within a configuration rather than random per process. It is irrelevant for ROC-AUC at the scale of any difference this chapter interprets, but it can still matter for P@$N$, which changes discontinuously when two companies swap positions around the ranking cut-off.

A relative numerical change as small as ($10^{-15}$) can change P@$500$ by 0.004. For this reason, the report does not treat P@$N$ differences below about 0.01 as meaningful results.

Voluntary exit provides an extreme example: 982 of the top 1,000 scores are exact ties. This happens because the boosted model assigns the same leaf value to very large groups of companies.

As a result, P@$N$ is not really measuring a fine ranking among those companies. It partly depends on which companies the partition procedure happens to select from the tied group.

The two rare targets behave differently. Their score gaps around rank 500 are around ($10^{-4}$), so numerical noise is not enough to change their ordering.

\subsubsection{The paired bootstrap}

The paired bootstrap is important when comparing two models. We know that, both models are evaluated on the same companies at the same origins. Therefore, much of the uncertainty caused by the companies themselves is shared by both models. Hence, if we calculate separate confidence intervals for each model and simply ask whether the intervals overlap, we ignore this shared variation. This makes the test unnecessarily conservative. Instead, we resample the rows once and calculate both models' metrics on exactly the same resample. The difference between the two models then becomes the quantity of interest. For the lending comparison, the separate intervals overlap heavily and suggest that the difference is unclear. The paired interval is about five times narrower and clearly excludes zero. The paired method is therefore the appropriate test because the question is not. Instead of asking ourselves if a different group of companies would produce different results, we must investigate if these same companies do receive different rankings under the two models.

\subsubsection{Why pooled P@$N$ is misleading}
P@$N$ calculated by combining several months is not the average of the monthly P@$N$ values. When the two test origins are pooled together, the pooled value falls outside the range of the two monthly values in 9 of 12 target-and-model-family combinations. In six of those nine cases, the difference is greater than 0.02.

This happens for two reasons.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item For lending, pooling makes the result look better. The pooled precision is 0.282, compared with monthly values of 0.218 and 0.226. This happens because selecting the best 500 companies from two months means selecting less deeply from each individual monthly ranking.
    \item For voluntary exit, pooling makes the result look worse. The pooled value is 0.296, compared with monthly values of 0.404 and 0.450. This can happen because model scores are not necessarily directly comparable across different origins. When the months are pooled, the top 500 can become dominated by whichever month happens to have higher absolute scores, rather than by the companies that are best ranked within their own month.
\end{enumerate}

Therefore, per-origin P@$N$ is the honest operational metric. It also matches how a RM actually receives the output: one month's ranking at a time.

The pooled metric is kept only when comparing runs in a way that treats both runs identically.

\subsubsection{Final observations}
All of these problems have the same underlying pattern.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A check was run, it produced a clean-looking result, and the result was initially believed.
    \item The pooled number looked like an average.
    \item The tied top-500 looked like a real ranking.
    \item The bootstrap looked like a complete confidence interval.
    \item  The hyperparameter search produced a confident and internally consistent ranking of twelve configurations, identical in its winner across three targets, and that ranking does not survive contact with the model we deploy (\S\ref{sec:pipeline}).
\end{enumerate}

None of these calculations was mathematically wrong. The problem was that we interpreted them as answering questions that they did not actually answer.

The same issue appeared in the blind-replay test in \S\ref{sec:leak}, in the acceptance gate discussed in \S\ref{sec:limits}, and in the hyperparameter search of \S\ref{sec:pipeline}, which scored every candidate at a fixed round count that the production fit never reaches. This is the main process lesson we learned from the modelling: a test is not useful simply because it runs and returns a clean result. We also need to check that the test is capable of detecting the failure it is supposed to detect.

One technical limitation was found late and closed. The staged runner originally persisted only the boosted model, so for most of the project only boosted comparisons could be paired, and the linear verdict of §\ref{sec:n4} rested on unpaired point estimates. The runner now writes the held-out labels, the row keys and one score vector per model family for every target, which costs roughly 30 MB a target and about 120 MB a run rather than the few megabytes we first assumed. Every future comparison between any two runs and any model family is therefore pairable by construction, and the comparison that motivated the change was re-measured directly from the stored matrices, in §\ref{sec:n4}.

\subsection{The synthesis}

News media does not accurately provide meaningful coverage of companies at this size. The statutory record indentifies them effectively but only after a serious problem has occurred, which is also too late for a bank seeking early prediction. Neither of the sources provides an effective early warning signal. We measured this negative finding through testing rather than just assuming, and it provides the main reason for using structured company register data as the foundation of the forecasting model instead of relying on text based information.

\footnotetext{Lift represents how many times better the model's top 100 is than random picking. It's precision divided by base rate.}

The most important metric of the table is lift; voluntary exit is the worked example of why. One might think it looks like the best result with 85\% correct precision. However, it is the weakest of the four, because roughly one company in 12 proposes a strike-off within six months anyway. So, a list picked at random from the register would already be right about 80\% of the time. 

On the other hand, lending readiness at 43\% is the stronger result the project produced on a clean run. This is because it operates at an extremely low base rate: with the event it is predicting happening to about one company in 400, getting 43 correct calls in 100 against that background is a huge improvement, and the one we most strongly present commercially. A newly registered charge is a financing event with a decision behind it, and a company that is about to make that decision is one worth speaking to first for Lloyds.

In terms of how sound the 4 target approach is, we have measured it. Across the 400 rows (4 shortlist, one per target x top 100 companies = 400 rows total) there are 398 unique companies. There are two appearing twice, both on Lending Readiness and Growth Signal, but this is economically sensible given that a company that's growing is exactly the kind of company that also needs to borrow to fund that growth, so the overlap isn't random noise. That is the operational payoff of the decision in \S\ref{sec:targets} to build four separate indices rather than one composite prospect score: the four carve the book into nearly non-overlapping groups rather than re-ranking the same few thousand companies four times.

We need however to flag a limitation here: the voluntary exit ranking should not be treated as a
ranking of individuals. Its top 1000 scores contain only fourteen distinct values, and 138
companies are tied at exactly the score sitting in 100th place, so "the top 100" there is an
arbitrary hundred drawn from a pool of more than two hundred equally ranked companies, and
re-running the sort could return a different hundred with identical scores. In other words, the model can't tell apart the companies at the top of its list; it lumps them into a few big groups with identical scores instead of ranking them one by one. The other three targets do not have this problem: their top 1000 hold 993, 961 and 994 distinct scores, with a single company at the cut.

\subsection{One company, end to end}
Because a ranked list is not the deliverable and a scored, explained row is. Our worked example throughout Chapter 3 is a Small manufacturer, about ten years old, active in all thirty-three panel months. It took on new secured borrowing three separate times inside the window, it was a Lloyds client at the start of the panel and released both Lloyds charges in November 2023 before borrowing from two competitors, and in February 2026 it won its first public-sector contract, a £597k award. \subsection{Two sources whose limits are structural}

\textbf{Trade Marks} achieved 1.05 percent coverage but the dataset itself has major limitations: the register ends on 28 January 2018. The intellectual Property Office released the free bulk dataset in February of that year and has not been updated since. This limitations comes from the source itself rather than the collection process, meaning any recent activity measure based on it cannot exist.

\textbf{Hiring data} cannot be expanded effectively. Adzuna searches jobs using keywords and locations rather than employer names. Therefore, finding a specific small company requires donwloading a national job feed and hoping that the company appears within it. \subsection{Two defects found in our own collection code}

We include these issues because both created zero results that could have been mistaken for genuine findings. The trade mark matching process produced no matches during its first run. The problem was caused by comparing full CH postcodes with IPO postcode areas, such as comparing \texttt{LU1 3AB} with \texttt{LU1}. These values could never match , and the error did not generate any warning. The grant collection process had also never sucessfully run because it used an incorrect API endpoint that returned a 404 error, while the correct address differed by only one path segment. A dataset returning no results because of technical error is impossible to distinguish from a dataset containing no relevant information by output alone. \section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
The limitations below are listed plainly without softening. Naming them makes the work more trustworthy. Most of them are recorded openly in the project's own run log.

\paragraph{A permanent gap in the source}
CH never published a snapshot for June 2025 \cite{companieshouse_api}. The is a permanent gap, not a download error. The pipeline was built to cope with it, but it still leaves a mark for the month that sits right  next to the gap, most of all a one-month change measured at July 2025, those features comes back blank rather than being guessed.

\paragraph{The growth model's feature count was, for a while, misleading}
A bug in the growth pipeline meant nine  of its features were completely empty in every training month, even though they had values in the test months. A model cannot learn from a column it never sees while training, so the growth model was really  using far fewer features than its label claimed. Those columns were removed, taking the nominal featured from a forty-one featured to twenty-seven it actually uses, and is reported honestly.

\paragraph{Secured lending does not show the full relationship}
Features used in this project show who has registered a loan against a company, but that captures secured borrowing only. It says nothing about current accounts, deposits, unsecured loans or advice, so it is a narrow view of the real relationship. A fuller picture would need informtion the public record does not hold and it is well established that non-financial and behavioral data add real value beyong any single record like this \cite{altman_sabato_wilson_2010,berg_etal_2020}

\paragraph{Limited population}
the modelling data was deliberately not restricted to actively trading firms. It keeps every company status, so that failed companies supply the insolvency labels the model learns from and it includes roughly 181,000 dormant companies rather than dropping them. But the two boundaries remain.The population is filtered to project's three target sectors about 1.5 mln companies of the roughly 5.7 mln on the register, which is smaller slice of the whole UK business population \cite{beis_bpe}. So the findings are specific to those sectors. Live scores are produced only for active companies because a lending ot growth forecast for a company already is liquidation is meaningless. Extending scoring to a wider deployed population is a natural next step.

\section{Contributions}
\note{Re-summarise, in the order they matter. A reproducible 33-month panel over
the whole UK register with a run registry behind it. Four self-labelled targets
and a ranked, explainable shortlist that routes to the three teams the client
named. Five measured negatives that most projects would have left unmeasured.
And a methodological contribution that is genuinely transferable beyond this
domain: the distinction between when a fact happened and when it became visible,
and what it costs to get that wrong.}
The project makes four contributions.

First, a reproducible pipeline from the UK public register to a modelling matrix. We assembled
thirty-three monthly Companies House snapshots into a company-month panel of 49.6 million rows
covering the whole register, with every feature defined once in a shared package and every model run
recorded so that any result is reproducible.

Second, four self-labelled prediction targets and a ranked, explainable shortlist. Because the data
is a time series, each target is a future value of a column we already hold, so no hand labelling is
required. Every score carries a SHAP explanation, and the four rankings are routed by counterparty to
the three teams the client named, so the output is a set of lists each team can act on rather than one
undifferentiated ranking.

Third, five results that came back negative and were kept because they were measured rather than
assumed. Narrative news media does not reach companies of this size, the statutory insolvency record
reaches them only after failure, the additional lender features give the deployed model nothing, the
name-keyed external sources reach almost none of the population, and hyperparameter tuning did not
improve on the shipped defaults. Most projects would have left these untested, and measuring them is
what tells a bank where not to spend its next effort.

Fourth, a methodological contribution that transfers beyond this domain. The project turns on the
distinction between when a fact happened and when it became visible. A charge created on one date but
delivered and registered later must not enter a feature row until it was actually observable, and
getting that boundary wrong produced a model that looked almost perfect while secretly reading its own
label. Separating the event clock from the visibility clock, and testing against the latter, applies
to any predictive system built on records that arrive after the events they describe.

\section{Further work}
\note{Focus on the interesting openings rather than on a wish list. The gate
gradient has not flattened at the tightest margin we tested, so the leak-free
intercept may be lower still and a tighter rung would settle it. Persist
held-out prediction vectors per model family, since the verdict rests partly on
logistic gains we cannot paired-test. Widen the modelling population to include
Fading and Insolvent companies so the statutory signal can inform the model
rather than decorate it. And the enrichment loop that we parked: rank the top
500 per target, enrich all of them, re-run, now that the ranking half costs one
command and only the API cost per company is unknown.}

Rather than a wish list, four openings stand out, each following from a result already in hand.

The calibrated lender gate is the tightest we tested, yet the lending effect is still increasing at
that margin, so the truly leak-free point may sit tighter still. A single tighter gate rung would show
where the gradient levels off and settle the leak-free intercept.

The comparison between model families rests in part on logistic-regression gains we could not test on
paired samples, because only summary metrics were retained. Persisting the held-out prediction vector
for each model family on every run would allow a paired bootstrap between families and turn those gains
from suggestive into tested.

The modelling population should be widened. At present the models are scored on Active companies only,
so the register's own record of distress decorates the data rather than informing the model. Including
the Fading and Insolvent companies in the modelling population, not only at scoring time, would let
that record of failure contribute to what the model learns about risk.

Finally, the targeted enrichment loop can now be run cheaply. The intention is to rank the top five
hundred companies per target, enrich all of them with the deeper API lookups, and re-run. Because the
ranking half of that loop now costs a single command, the only unknown left is the API cost per
company, and closing the loop would complete the structured-first, enrich-narrow design the project
set out to build.
