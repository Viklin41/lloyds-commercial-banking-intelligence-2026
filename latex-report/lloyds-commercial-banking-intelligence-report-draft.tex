\chapter*{Abstract}
% Compulsory, at most 1 page. Owner: TBC, written LAST.
\note{One page. The problem (Lloyds needs to find BB/SME prospects and risks
among companies that are not already clients), what we built (a 33-month

targets, eleven recorded model runs, an explainable ranked shortlist), and what
we found (the structured record predicts; narrative media does not reach
companies of this size at all, and the statutory record reaches them only after

A commercial bank has full visibility of its own customers and very little of companies it does not serve. Working with lloyds Banking Group's Commercial Banking division, this project asks whether the public record alone can identify business and SME prospects and risks among both its existing customers and companies that are not yet clients, using only freely available data and no internal information. The question is framed as a search over large and sparse population rather than a classification over a known customer base and is scoped to roughly 1.4 million SMEs across three target sectors.

To answer it, we reconstructed a history the register does not provide, assembling thirty-three monthly snapshots of Companies House into a company month panel of 49.6 million rows. On this panel we engineered 41 features across four families and defined four self-labelled targets, each read from a future state of the same register so that the labels are learnable without look ahead. We then compared an interpretable baseline against a gradient boosted tree and a neural network over eleven recorded model runs, and attached a SHAP explanation to every score, so that the output is ranked and defensible shortlist with a reason for each entry.

The project's central finding is that the structured public record does carry predictive signal, with the lending readiness shortlist ranks companies at roughly one hundred times the base rate among its top candidates. Unstructured news, articles and media however, does not reach companies of this size at all, and when its coverage of target population was measured it was negligible, so unstructured signals was added for companies flagged with signals. The statutory record of insolvency describes a company only after it has failed, to late to act on. Establishing these boundaries with evidence is contribution in its own right, because it direct effort toward the sources that work and away from those that cannot.

\noindent
In quantitative terms the project delivered the following
\begin{itemize}
    \item a 33-month company month panel of 49.6 million rows over the Companies House register;
    \item 41 engineered feature across four families and four self-labelled prediction targets;
    \item an elevn run model comparison across three model families;
    \item a 25.4 hour programmatic harvest of 558{,}693 secured charges;
    \item a 284{,}230 notice crawl of \emph{The Gazette};
    \item a data leakage defect identified and correct over four passes;
    \item and a lending shortlist running at roughly one hundred times the base rate among its top 500 candidates
\end{itemize}

LLoyds Banking Group's Commercial Banking division provides lending, transactional banking, working-capital management, debt financing and risk-management services to business\cite{lloyds_products}, operating through LLoyds Banking Group and its family of brands \cite{lbg_brands}. This project, carried out by a student team in collaboration with the bank, asks whether the public record alone including Companies Houses and other free sources, can identify commercial opportunites and risks among both existing customers and companies the bank currently does not serve, without any access to internal client data.

\section{The problem}
\label{sec:problem}
A commercial bank has complete visibility of its own customers and very little of those it does not serve, so the task is to identify, among roughly 5.7 million companies on the UK register \cite{companieshouse_api}, those likely to need lending, those showing early signs of distress and growth opportunites, explicitly including companies that do not bank with LLoyds. This is therefore a search problem over a large and highly sparse population, in which the events of interest are rare and most of the population is invisible to the bank today, rather than classification problem over  a known customer base. To make this tractable, scope of analysis was chosen to roughly 1.4 million companies targeting small and medium-sized  enterprises (SMEs) and sectors including manufacturing, legal and professional services, and fast-growth and emerging companies. These sectors were chosen because they were expected to have high proportion of SMEs rather than large corporates. Concretely, we ask whether structured public records can be turned into features, whether a model can use these features to rank companies for further commercial review of the news and articles regarding the flagged companies that shows relative signals and whether the resulting scores can be explained and linked to specific Lloyds services such as invoice finance, assest finance and working capital support\cite{lloyds_products}.



\section{Why it matters, and to whom}
\note{Three named audiences from the client brief: the Growth team (``not
ours''), the Attrition team (``ours and leaving''), the Maintenance team
(``ours and staying''). Say here that those three are defined by
\emph{counterparty}, not by need, which is why counterparty identity became a
workstream of its own and why the routing in \S\ref{sec:routing} is the piece
that turns a ranked list into something a person can act on.}


Small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) account for the majority of the UK businesses and employment \cite{beis_bpe}. Yet, they face persistent gaps in access to finance, which makes their banking relationships both commercially valuable and contested \cite{bbb_2024,oecd_2024_sme}. A bank that predicts need can earn two advantages, it can approach a prospect before a competitor does and it can flag a risk to an existing client early enough to act. Both matter because bank has little visibility of companies it does not serve and because early signs of change frequently appear in public filings before they appear anywhere internal. The project is carried out on an established idea that public and alternative data carry genuine value for financial outcomes. Corporate failure prediction from public financial signals is long studied  \cite{altman_1968} and recent work shows non-traditional, digital signals can predict credit outcomes competitively \cite{berg_etal_2020}. Our contribution is to apply this idea to specific commercial banking framing of growth, deepening existing relationship and attrition, using only free public data. We explain every model with SHAP \cite{lundberg_lee_2017}. The target users are the division's relationship managers, who gain evidence-led shortlist with a reason for each potential clients.

The brief is framed around three functions, and its worth being precise about them because they are the audiences for the output. The growth function focuses on companies that are not already Lloyds clients. Attrition focuses on existing clients who may be leaving Lloyds. Maintenance focuses on retaining existing customers. These groups are distinguished by counterparty, meaning which bank already serves the company and not by company's need the same company can be an opportunity for one team and risk for another depending on who currently holds its lending. So, one single ranked list of likely borrowers cannot support all three priorities. This is why identifying each company's counterparty, using CH charges API\cite{companieshouse_api}, became a workstream.

\section {Scope of the wider effort and of this thesis}
As a team we explored a broad range of free public sources beyond the brief's suggestion including the CH register and its APIs\cite{companieshouse_api}, public sector contract awards from Contracts Finder and Find a Tender \cite{contractsfinder,findatender,ocds}, secured lender identity from CH charges API \cite{companieshouse_api}, hiring, property and intellectual property signals \cite{adzuna_api, landregistry_ccod,ukipo_tm}, unstructured news and sentiment \cite{gdelt, newsapi,guardian_api,gnews_api}, and 33-month historical company panel. This pipeline is structured data first including structured signals and shortlist companies, and unstructured media is retrieved only for companies a signal has already flagged, keeping an expensive and  sparse lookup targeted rather than run across the whole population.

\section{Central challenges and how we mitigated it}

\note{ (i) Scale: 5.7M companies x 33 months is 49.6M rows on a laptop.
(ii) Time: the register is a photograph of now, so history had to be
reconstructed from 33 separate snapshots. (iii) Labels: nobody hands you a
label, so every target is self-labelled from a future state of the same
register, which makes leakage the central methodological risk. (iv) Identity:
public sources name companies four different ways and only some of them use the
company number. (v) \textbf{The signal we expected was not there.} Point
forward to N1, N2 and N5. Sneha's draft currently reads as though all four
challenges were surmounted; the fifth needs adding, because it is the finding
the report is built on.}

Four challenges were important to solve and fifth became a finding of project itself.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Coverage versus depth :} The bulk file covers every company but is shallow, while the APIs are rich but rate-limited, so features must be cheap at population scale with deeper information for a shortlist. We computed the population scale features directly from the bulk file at zero cost, and rather than sample the expensive API work we ran it to completion, keeping the CH rate limit of roughly two requests per second with automatic retries on errors and every response was cached, so the code ran for about 25 hours and returned the full data because we did not want to compromise on coverage \cite{companieshouse_api}.
    
    \item \textbf{Choosing what to predict :} CH records a company's status only as it stands now, not the date it changed, which makes a clean target hard to define and creates a leakage risk when the label and the features are drawn from the same moment, so a model can learn from after effects of failure rather than early warning signs. We addressed this by self labelling every target from a strictly future state of register, taking features from month $t$ and labels from $t{+}1$ onwards. Later, a automated leakage test was run to confirm that no features reaches forward.

    \item \textbf{Relationship gap :} Public data describes the company, not its banking relatinship. We can estimate need, but we cannot know from public data alone which bank already serves the company. To narrow this gap, we recovered counterparty identity from CH charges API by reading \texttt{persons\_entitled} lender on each charge and classifying it as Lloyds brand or a compitator using auditable rule dictionary \cite{companieshouse_api}. This is the closest proxy for the banking relationship.

    \item \textbf{Reliable linkage :} Matching a record to the correct company is error prone, so we joined on the CH number only, the  \texttt{GB-COH}, padded to common key and never on company name. A name and postcode match was added as a separate, precision tested extension. We attempted unstructured enrichment only on a small, justified shortlist to keep the risk of mislinkage low.

    \item \textbf{The expected signal was absent :} The unstructured news and media sentiment \cite{gdelt,newsapi,guardian_api,gnews_api} was built and measured, and its coverage of target population proved negligible. We measured coverage before commiting and when the signals proved absent, we pivoted. This negative result is a finding in its own right and it is the reason the final system was built on structured public records and the unstructured information was added as an enrichment layer for the flagged companies.

    
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Background}
\label{chap:background}
% 6 pages. Owner: Sneha, Viktor, Sam

\section{Papers: Predicting corporate distress and corporate demand}
The classical approach to predicting company failure is built on financial ratios. The best known example is Altman's Z-score \cite{altman_1968}. It is a statistical model that takes a small set of accounting ratios and combines them using weights estimated by discriminant analysis, into a single number the 'Z-score'. That number places a company in a 'safe', 'grey' or 'distress' zone, a low score flags a firm as likely to fail. Because it is simple, interpretable and only needs figures from published accounts, it remains the reference point for corporate distress models. The difficulty is that it assumes those accounts are available and comparable. Small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), which make up the bulk of the UK company register are entitled to file abbreviated or 'micro entity' accounts, which only require a balance sheet and a few short notes, not a full profit and loss statement or detailed financial breakdown \cite{companieshouse_accounts_2026}. For these firms the ratios that drive a Z-score are either missing or too coarse to be useful. This bears directly on our work, because one of the four outcomes we set out to predict is exactly this kind of financial distress, defined in \S\ref{sec:targets}. The Z-score is therefore the natural starting point for that target, and the fact that it cannot reach the SMEs at the centre of our brief is precisely the gap the rest of this chapter sets out to close.

A second line of work responds to this gap by turning to non-financial and behavioural information. Altman, Sabato and Wilson \cite{altman_sabato_wilson_2010} show that non-financial signals materially improve SME default prediction over accounts only models and later credit scoring research finds that behavioral and transactional data can perform as well as or even better than traditional financial variables \cite{berg_etal_2020}. This motivates our own emphasis on register derived behvaiour such as charges, director changes, filing activity rather than on reported financial data alone.

Almost all of this literature predicts distress which firms will fail. The complementary question which firms are about to need finance and ready to take it on is less studied, even though it is the question a commercial lender most wants answered. This 'demand side' framing is what distinguishes our project from conventional default model.

Companies House (CH) is the UK's register of companies, an executive agency sponsored by the Department for Business and Trade, responsible for incorporating, maintaining and dissolving limited companies and for examining and publishing the information they file \cite{companieshouse_api}. We access this record in two ways \cite{companieshouse_api}. \section{The modelling background: Gradient boosting, ranking, and explanation}
\label{sec:modelling-backgroud}
\note{Why gradient-boosted trees on tabular data with heavy missingness; why we
rank rather than classify (a relationship manager works a list, not a decision
boundary), which makes ROC-AUC and precision@$N$ the right pair of metrics and
accuracy the wrong one; and SHAP, both what it computes and why we need it, since
a shortlist without a reason attached to each row cannot be acted on. Keep the
maths light but present: an examiner should be able to read the SHAP figures.}

Here we outline the modelling choiches that shape everything later reported in Chapter \S\ref{chap:evaluation}; each of them follows from a property of the data: the model family, the framing of the task as ranking, and the requirement that every score comes with a reason attached.

\subsection{The model family follows the shape of the matrix}
What we assemble in Chapter \S\ref{chap:execution} is a wide tabular matrix of roughly 40 columns per company-month: counts, ratios, boolean flags and two categorical fields, with a great deal of missingness that is informative rather than accidental. A NULL twelve-month delta means the company was not on the register a year earlier; a NULL recency means the event has never happened at all. Gradient-boosted decision trees \cite{4a848dd1-54e3-3c3c-83c3-04977ded2e71, ke2017lightgbm} are the natural fit for exactly this shape. They fit an additive ensemble of shallow trees, each fitted to the gradient of the loss of the ensemble so far.  Three of their properties matter here more than their accuracy does:
\begin{itemize}
    \item They split on rank rather than on magnitude, so a procurement framework carrying a notional ceiling of one hundred and fifty billion pounds is treated as a large value rather than as an outlier that distorts a fit.
    \item They need no scaling and no encoding of ordinal structure.
    \item And they route missing values down a learned branch, so "we cannot compute this" is a value the model can act on rather than a hole that has to be filled with a mean the company does not have.
\end{itemize}
   The alternatives fail on the same three points. A regularised linear model cannot form the interactions that a size band and a charge count jointly imply, and it has to be handed an imputed value which it then fits a weight to, which is a claim about a company that our data does not make. A dense feed-forward network has to learn the mixed types, the missingness convention and the sharp regulatory thresholds from scratch, through a one-hot layer, from a few hundred thousand rows. 
   
   We fit both anyway: the linear model as an interpretable floor, because a boosted ensemble that cannot clearly beat it is not earning its complexity, and the network because it is the honest occupant of the complex end of the interpretability trade-off this section is about. Their role is comparative rather than competitive.

\subsection{The task is ranking, not classification}
A relationship manager works a finite call list. Nobody has asked us for a decision boundary, and inventing one would substitute our threshold for their capacity. So the deliverable is an ordering of companies by probability, and the evaluation is an evaluation of an ordering. That decides the metrics, and it decides them against the obvious choice. At a base rate near one in three hundred, a model that predicts "no" for every company on the register is more than ninety-nine per cent accurate and completely worthless; accuracy on this problem is not merely weak, it is actively misleading, and the same objection disqualifies any single-threshold statistic such as recall or F1 unless the threshold is itself justified. For this reason, we report two metrics instead, because they answer two different questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item ROC-AUC asks whether the ordering holds globally: draw a positive and a negative at random, how often is the positive ranked above it.
    \item Precision@$N$ asks the operational question: of the $N$ companies we actually put in front of somebody, how many were right.
\end{itemize}
A model can be respectable on the first and useless on the second, and, as \S\ref{sec:leak} shows, we used reverse combination a diagnostic of leakage. Keeping both, at several values of $N$, is cheap insurance and it is what made our own leakage defect visible within a minute of looking at the table. 
One reading instruction belongs with precision@$N$ from the outset: it must be judged against the base rate it sits on, not on its face. A precision of 0.85 on a target that fires for eight per cent of companies anyway is a weaker result than a precision of 0.43 on a target that fires for one in four hundred. The second is roughly a hundred and fifty times better than chance, and the first is about ten. In Chapter \S\ref{chap:evaluation} we report the lift column alongside the precision column for precisely this reason.

\subsection{Every score comes with a reason}
A ranked list is not yet a deliverable: the person receiving it has to open a conversation, and "the model placed you 499th" is not an opening. We therefore attach per-company attributions using SHAP \cite{lundberg_lee_2017}, which adapts the Shapley value from cooperative game theory \cite{shapley1953value}. The features of a single row are treated as players in a game whose payout is that row's prediction, and each feature is credited with its average marginal contribution over all orderings in which the players could have arrived: 

$$\phi_j(x) \;=\; \sum_{S \subseteq F \setminus \{j\}} \frac{|S|!\,(|F|-|S|-1)!}{|F|!}\Big[f\big(S \cup \{j\}\big) - f(S)\Big].$$ 

This is the unique attribution satisfying local accuracy, missingness and consistency, and the property we actually spend is local accuracy: the contributions sum to the prediction, so a composite index can be decomposed into the things that drove it rather than merely correlated with it. The cost of computing that varies enormously across the three families, and the spread is a result in its own right rather than an implementation detail:
\begin{itemize}
    \item For a linear model the Shapley value collapses to $\phi_j(x) = \beta_j\,(x_j - \mathbb{E}[x_j])$, a rescaling of a coefficient that is already printed on the fitted model, so a simple model does not need an explainer at all and we read ours through its coefficients.
    \item For a tree ensemble, \texttt{TreeExplainer} \cite{lundberg_etal_2020} exploits the tree structure to recover the exact values in low-order polynomial time, which is seconds for a hundred thousand rows. That cheapness raises an obvious objection to the rule we are applying, and it is worth answering here rather than leaving it to the reader: if the explanation is exact and effectively free, is the ensemble complex enough to need one at all? It is, and the reason is that cheapness of explanation and self-explanation are different properties. A several-hundred-tree ensemble has no readable parameter vector, so unlike the linear model there is no coefficient to substitute for the attribution; the explainer is not a convenience there, it is the only route to a reason.
    \item For a dense network there is no such shortcut, and the model-agnostic \texttt{KernelExplainer} must repeatedly perturb each row and refit a local weighted linear surrogate, at roughly a thousand times the cost and with a sampling error of its own. That spread points at a conclusion sharper than the textbook trade-off. The usual framing is that accuracy is bought with interpretability, and each is the price of the other.
\end{itemize}

What the three points above actually describe is a spectrum on which the middle point is not a compromise: the boosted ensemble is complex in the way this data is complex, and it also happens to be the one family whose explanations are exact and effectively free. The network pays the full interpretability price without collecting a corresponding accuracy reward on tabular data of this shape. Chapter \S\ref{chap:evaluation} tests that claim on our own matrix rather than asserting it, and the answer it returns is the reason the deployed model is the boosted one. 

We use SHAP rather than LIME \cite{ribeiro2016whyitrustyou} for two reasons that matter at delivery rather than in principle:
\begin{itemize}
    \item For trees, SHAP is exact rather than a local surrogate fitted to sampled neighbours
    \item and it is stable across re-runs rather than resampled, which is not a statistical nicety when the same company may be shown to the same person twice.
\end{itemize} 

Finally, one caution that Chapter 4 develops properly. Every metric named here is a point estimate computed on one held-out sample, and a comparison between two of them is a comparison of two point estimates. Nothing in this project is reported without an interval, and \S\ref{sec:errors} sets out what those intervals do and do not cover, which turns out to differ by model family.

\section{Public data sources beyond the register}

The register only shows what a company has officially submitted and does not show the activites a company has carried out. If a business purcheses a warehouse, secures a local authority contract, registers a trademark or recieves public funding for research, this will leave evidence in other public databases and these records are not included in the Companies House bulk file. In this section we review the external sources we used to find this additional information.

Each source identifies a company in either one of two main ways and the difference between this affects the quality of the results strongly as discussed in \S\ref{sec:sources-eval}. Some sources provide the Companies House registration number, meaning that we can match the companies directly using exact string matching after both values are formatted as eight characters. The other sources only provide a company name, meaning that we must identify the company through name matching, which is a probability based process rather than a simple lookup. For example, an Oxford compay named "Amazon Ltd" is not necessarily connected to Amazon Web Services. Adding details such as postcodes can improve matching the accuracy but it does not exactly guarantee the correct results, especially when those details are incomplete.

\paragraph{Keyed on the number}. \textbf{HM Land Registry Commercial and Corporate Ownership Data} \cite{landregistry_ccod} contains information about freehold and leasehold properties in England and Wales owned by United Kingdom companies. This covers around 3.5 million property titles including registration numbers for company owners in four possible properietor fields. Information on property ownership provides useful information about assets that could potentially support decisions regarding whether they should be lent to. \textbf{Contracts Finder} and \textbf{Find a Tender} provide public sector contract awards through OCDS feeds, where in supplier identifiers are available frequently enough to support reliable matching. \textbf{The Gazette} acts as the official source for insolvency notices. It does not provide a direct company search and only includes details such as category, date , and postcode, meaning that we must collect notices in bulk and match them using the company number which is included in within the notice text. \textbf{GLEIF} \cite{gleif} and \textbf{Wikidata} allows exact connections between Legal Entity Identifiers and company records, although they mainly cover organisations involved in financial markets.

\paragraph{Keyed on a name.} The \textbf{Intellectual Property Office} trademark database \cite{ukipo_tm} provides the owner's name and only the first part of the postcode such as \texttt{SL3} instead of the full \texttt{SL3 9EH}, and does not include any registration number. \textbf{UK Research and Innovation}'s Gateway to Research \cite{ukri_gtr} contains information about publicly funded research projects. Its organization structure includes a \texttt{regNumber} field, which could have allowed exact matching, but this field is not populated. \textbf{Adzuna} \cite{adzuna_api} provides us with employer names and locations but cannot be searched directly by employer, only through keywords and location filters. \textbf{News API'S}, including the Gurdian Open Platform \cite{guardian_api}, NewsAPI, GNews, and GDELT, provides unstructured text where companies are mentioned in the style used by journalists often without legal suffixes or location detials. \textbf{Listed company financials} use stock tickers as identifiers and we include them only as a recorded approach that was not suitable for our project goal.

\paragraph{Why an entity spine was necessary.} Since many sources identify companies only by its name, it is not possible to search each database independently and reliably. So to solve this we created an entity called a spine: a central table containing one record per company with its standardised name, previous name, postcode and any external identifiers that could be linked back to it. This ensures that we make all matches against one consistent company list, stored only once with a confidence score that can be reused by future users. We describe the process fo creating this entity spine in \S\ref{sec:unstructured-method}

\subsection{The initial June 2026 snapshot}
As mentioned, we started experimenting with our approach on the June 2026 bulk product from CH. CH is an executive agency, sponsored by the UK Department for Business and Trade. As stated on their official website, their responsibilities are to "incorporate, maintain and dissolve limited companies... examine and publish company information" \cite{companieshouse_api}. This is crucial to understand as it explains how we could access this data in the first place. A company must file annual accounts, a confirmation statement once a year restating its basic details, and other statements on an event basis. All of this is public and enabled us to create the data basis to run our modelling. 

Once we obtained the June snapshot comprising the 5,698,276 companies, we decided to narrow it down for initial testing. We utilised the official Standard Industrial Classification codes list \cite{companieshouse_sic}, scraping the full 731 codes. From this, we mapped 325 of them onto our 3 target sectors: Technology, Legal \& Professional, Manufacturing and Fast growth \& emerging. See \ref{app:sic-specific-codes} for more details on how the mapping logic works. We also filtered to \texttt{CompanyStatus == "Active"} and tiered by size based on \texttt{CompanyNumber} \cite{companieshouse_accounts_2026}. This left us with 1,372,321 companies.

The join key we used here and for every table in this report is stated once here and never restated: \texttt{CompanyNumber}, zero-padded to eight digits. 

Here it is worth noting that the register maintained by CH records a company's \textbf{status} (active, insolvent, etc.), but does not carry any information on when or why it switched to a particular status. For this reason, we later incorporate data from the \textit{Gazette}, the UK's official public record, which publishes formal notices relating to corporate insolvency. These notices cover a range of insolvency events, including administrations, receiverships, voluntary liquidations, winding-up petitions and winding-up orders, and can contain event-specific information such as the date on which a petition was filed and the date on which a winding-up order was made \cite{gazette_insolvency}. This makes The Gazette particularly valuable for reconstructing the sequence of events preceding and surrounding insolvency, rather than relying only on a company's observed status in the CH register. This distinction is important as it explains why every target in \S\ref{sec:targets} is defined as an event occurring within a forward window rather than as a state observed at a particular point in time.



\subsection{Acquiring the historical data}
We quiclky incurred a limitation as the single snapshot described a company today, but did not express change, which is essential to encapsulate the modelling signal. More fundamentally, a single snapshot contains limitations on what to predict as it has no time-linked target event. For this reason, we successfully attempted to create a time series dataset, solving both problems at once and turning the target label into a future value of a column we already hold.

To do so, we dug deeper into the CH website and found older monthly .zips stored on the server, reachable by direct URL. These file names are not reliably dated to the first of the month, so rather than guessing, we probed days 1 to 15 of each candidate month once and recorded the dates that resolved. This gave us a manifest of 33 snapshots from October 2023 to July 2026. We keep all 33 zips, roughly 15 GB, permanently: the server ages older months off, at which point our copy is the only one, and re-deriving the panel with a different column set then costs no downloads at all.

While doing this operation, we encountered two methodological considerations that changed our design moving forward:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The panel is built in two passes rather than one. The obvious approach is to replay the cross-sectional filter on every month independently. That is wrong, because a company can be re-coded out of our target sectors without dying, and under a per-month filter that company simply disappears from the panel, which is indistinguishable from a dissolution. So pass one collects every \texttt{CompanyNumber} matching our sectors in any month, and pass two emits a row for every member of that universe present in a given month, whatever its status or current sector. Absence from the panel now means one thing only: gone from the register. The \texttt{recoded\_out\_of\_sector} column records how often this mattered.
    \item The second is that the panel keeps every status. Filtering to Active (as we did in the first filtering logic for practicality), replayed monthly, would delete every failure event and produce a panel in which no company ever fails, leaving a credit-risk model with nothing to learn from. Insolvent companies are not prospects, but they are precisely the labels we need. They stay in the panel, and we apply \texttt{is\_active} at scoring time instead. Under an Active-only build the non-Active share would read zero in all 33 months; in the panel it rises steadily, which is exactly the signal \S\ref{sec:targets} keys off.
    \item A third, smaller decision protects point-in-time correctness at build time: the static feature computation is passed the real snapshot date rather than being allowed to default to the current date, so company age, accounts-overdue and accounts-stale reflect what was true in that month rather than what is true whenever the code happens to run.
\end{enumerate}

This results in a panel holding 49,556,152 rows over roughly 2.04 million companies, stored long rather than wide: one row per company per month, so a company's history is read down the rows rather than across the columns. We saved the bulk dataset as 33 parquet files keyed on the month start, with the true extract date carried alongside as \texttt{source\_date}. Both choiches matter later. The long format means adding a feature adds a column and never a row. TBC 

Before moving forward we performed two checks to determine the historical correctness of our files. First, we took the panel's June 2026 partition, filtered it back to Active and in-sector, and verified it landed exactly on the cross-sectional build, at
1,372,321 rows with an identical company set and identical sector counts (Tech and legal 837,066, Fast growth 320,670, Manufacturing 214,585). It did. Since the other 32 months are
built by the same code path, that one match is what licenses the rest. Secondly, we tested for point-in-time correctness. We followed individual companies across different monthly snapshots. Their recorded age should increase by roughly one year for every 12 months that pass. If their age stayed constant, that would mean the dataset was incorrectly using today's date instead of the date of each historical snapshot. We confirmed that this problem did not occur.

While downloading the monthly snapshots we encountered a significant limitation. CH never published a snapshot for June 2025. Rather than interpolate a month we do not have, we carry an explicit NULL row for it through a dense calendar spine, so the panel has 33 observed months across 34 calendar months. That decision propagates a long way. It is why every lookback in \S\ref{sec:features} is calendar-aware rather than positional and why every label window in \S\ref{sec:targets} uses a calendar range frame. It returns as a genuine limitation in \S\ref{sec:limits}.

\section{Structured Feature engineering: Companies House and additional source}
\note{Summarise `reports/dashboard_handover_columns.md`}
The team explored a wide range of public signals and not all of them were kept in the final model, this section records both what we kept and what we discarded along with the reasons. The retained features form four families, all keyed on eight digit \texttt{CompanyNumber}.  Two rules run through every family, every feature is calculated strictly as at the month it belongs to. A feature for month t can only use information that existed in the month t, never anything from the future, which is what  lets \S\ref{sec:targets} define learnable targets. Every feature's definition is stored in a catalogue and turned into a short hash, a finger print of exact input and logic used. This means any two runs can be proven to have used identical outputs, giving reproducibility and preventing silent changes.

\paragraph{Group 1 - The company as it is now}
The first group describes each company as the register currently shows it and it is pulled straight from the Companies Houses bulk file \cite{companieshouse_api} with no extra lookups needed. It covers how old the company is, how many loans it has secured against its assets and how many are still outstanding, a simple debt ratio built from those, whether its accounts are overdue or out of date, a size band which is read from type of accounts it files \cite{companieshouse_accounts_2026}, the industry it works in from its SIC code \cite{companieshouse_sic} and whether it has changed its name. Many of these were prototyped in attrition work, company health read from its status, a dormant flag, a rough size band and late filings warning and checked against early static exploration before comvinign in a single panel.

\paragraph{Group 2 - how the company is changing}
A single snapchat cannot show the changes over the years, and the changes is exactly the signal we mostly want. A company whose secured borrowing has jumped in six months was behaving very differntly from one that has sat still for years, even if the two look identical in the present. So thirty three month history was assembled and measured the change over the last three, six and twelve months. The change in charge counts, in outstanding charges, in the debt ratio and how many separate new-borrowing events happened in the past year was recorded.

One considerable gap in the source shaped the entire design, Companies House never published a snapshot for June 2025 . This is  a permanent gap in their published data, the server was re-checked and the file has never existed, not a download that could be simply repeated. So 'going back three rows' in history to measure the change, could have been landed on May 2025 instead of June 2025. It would have quietly reported a four month change as if it were three, without a warning. Instead every company was laid against a full run of calender months, so the missing month appears as an explicit blank and every look-back is exactly the right number of calendar months, reaching across the gap returns a blank rather than a wrong number. This group also carries the earliest warning sign in the public record such as features that track how logn since a company last filed its accounts or confirmation statement and how long its accounts have been stale. These indicators stay flat while a company is still filling normally and only start to rise once the company stops filing, which usually happens before its status officially changes to insolvency.

\paragraph{Group 3 - winning public work}
This register shows how a company files and borrows, but not whether its winning business. From the Contracts Finder and Find a Tender \cite{contractsfinder, findatender, ocds}, each company's public-sector contract wins were aggregated including the count, their total and average value, and whether it has just won its very first, matched using the Companies House number carried in contract record. Part-way through the window the rules changed. When Procurement Act 2023 came into force on 24 February 2025, new public contracts stopped being published on Contracts Finder and began appearing on Find a Tender \cite{findatender}, while Contracts Finder kept only older notices. So information from both were combined to avoid false drop in wins that has nothing to do with companies themselves. Every win is counted only up to the month in question, so 2026 award never lands on 2024 row.

\paragraph{Group 4 - who the company banks with}
For companies that have secured loans, lender names attached to each charge was read using Charges API \cite{companieshouse_api} , every name was sorted using a clear ordered rule list, into either "a Lloyds brand" (Lloyds Bank, Bank of Scotland, Halifax, HBOS and related names \cite{lbg_brands} or "a rival". This turns a raw public record into necessary information of who the company banks with, the very axis the client's growth and attrition questions are asked on. Two more relationship signals from Officiers and Filling History records were added, recent director appointments and resignations, and new-share (SH01) filings as a sign that company is raising money.

If no objection is raised, the company is subsequently struck off and dissolved. Thus, in this study, "a strike-off proposal appears" refers to the observable initiation of this voluntary dissolution process, rather than to the company already being dissolved. Voluntary strike-off is therefore interpreted as a signal of voluntary exit, rather than as a direct indicator of insolvency. See Companies House~\cite{companieshouse_strikingoff_2025}.}

\subsection{Distress logic evolution \& Growth signal segments  }
The original plan defined the distress label as Active at \texttt{t} and non-Active by \texttt{t+H}, this meant that if a company disappeared from the Actice category for any reason, it was laelled as a general distress. That runs at roughly four per cent, and about ninety per cent of it is voluntary strike-off: dormant micro-companies quietly closing. That is not a credit event, and we assumed no bank wants it in a risk model. We therefore narrowed the definition to genuine insolvency status, explicitly listing the ten relevant statuses rather than relying on text matching. This ensures that any new, unexpected status is flagged rather than silently missed. This dropped the rate to about 0.34\%. We model both outcomes, separately, as Credit Risk Exposure and Voluntary Exit, because they are different questions with different audiences and blending them lets the loud label drown the important one.

We compared the two outcomes to determine if this was the right. Restricted to rows where both labels are defined, a company that proposes a strike-off is less likely than average to enter genuine insolvency, at a lift of 0.59. The two also separate cleanly and in
opposite directions on company age: voluntary exit runs at 14.6\% for companies under two years old and falls to 3.1\% past twenty, while genuine insolvency runs at 0.05\% under two years and rises with age. Those are two different phenomena, a dormant shell being abandoned and an established trading business failing, and merging them would have produced a risk model predominantly about the former.

Delving deeper into this distinction, we measured whether a company was still sitting in "Proposal to Strike off" at exactly \texttt{t+6} and reported 3.4 to 3.8\%. The shipped label measures whether a proposal appeared at any month in the window and reports 6.7 to 8.5\%. Both numbers are correct about what they measure, and the entire gap is proposals raised and then withdrawn, which happens routinely once a company files its overdue accounts and accounts for about half of all proposals at every origin. We keep the event definition for two reasons. A proposal raised and withdrawn is still the moment a relationship manager wanted to know about, and it is specifically the half where intervention was still possible; defining it away because the company recovered would discard exactly those cases. And a state read off a single month is hostage to that month existing, which, given that June 2025 does not, is not a property we want any label to depend on. The honest cost is that Voluntary Exit means a strike-off process started, not that the company died. We flag this in the report as it requires caution when interpreting.

One further definitional care: the \texttt{segment} field mixes real size tiers with filing states such as Dormant and No Filings, so a naive "segment changed" test runs at about thirteen per cent over twelve months and is mostly filing churn rather than growth. The growth label therefore ranks only the four genuine tiers, and companies with no rank are excluded from the label rather than assigned to one.

\subsection{Quarterly Origins \& Train/Test Split design choices}
\label{sec:train-test-split-logic}
We decided to keep the the label origins quarterly rather than creating a training example for every month. This is done so each company contributes one observation every three months rather than a sequence of near-identical monthly observations, and the three-month lending windows do not overlap. This causes the six- and twelve-month targets to overlap by factors of two (6/3) and four (12/3) respectively. This is particularly dangerous for leakage as the same company can appear multiple times in training and validation if the split is performed randomly. For this reason we apply a grouped cross-validation in \S\ref{sec:pipeline}, splitting between training and validation by keeping the same company's observation across one or the other. Moreover, label windows are expressed as calendar range frames rather than positional offsets, for the same reason the delta features are: a positional frame would silently reach an extra month past the June 2025 hole. Finally, where a window contains no observed month at all the label is NULL and never zero, to separate unkown from unhappened.

The split we created follows \S\ref{sec:leakage-bg} directly: train on early origins, test on late ones, with an embargo of at least $H$ months between them so that training labels do not extend into test period. The quarterly grid pays off here. Lending has a three-month horizon and quarterly origins, so consecutive origins are already exactly $H$ apart and it loses nothing to the embargo, which is why it gets five training origins where the six-month targets get three. The twelve-month growth label pays the full price, losing three origins. 

To make things more robust, we implemented a guard, \texttt{FIRST\_FULL\_ORIGIN} (October 2024), that marks the first month when every feature has enough historical data to be properly formed rather than NULL for calendar reasons. However, if enforcing that rule would leave us with fewer than three training origins, we relax it rather than train on an unusably small sample. For the twelve-month target, this fallback always fires, which means that that target has less ideal training history than the other targets and the pipeline has to relax the guard to get enough training origins. This turns out to matter and is treated as a limitation in \S\ref{sec:limits} rather than as a design choice here.

\subsection{Negatives downsampling}
We faced a significant problem due to the targets scarcity. At a base rate near three in a thousand, most of 1.4 million rows per origin are negatives that teach a model nothing, and carrying them costs real computational time. For this reason, we kept every positive but downsampled the negative observations. Sampling is done per origin months so the origin mix is preserved for the out-of-time split described in \S\ref{sec:train-test-split-logic}, and negatives are selected by a hash of the company number rather than a random draw, which makes the sample reproducible without threading a seed through the query engine and more importantly keeps the choice consistent for a company across origins. The sampled positive rate lands at 1:10 for all four targets, which confirms it correctly downnsampled to the desired rate. We recorded the fraction of negative observations that we retained under the keep rate, which we carried as a column on every row. This is crucial as downsampling negatives at rate $r$ multiplies the odds by $1/r$ and inflates every predicted probability by roughly thirtyfold. While the probability ranking survives that, being a monotone transform; the numbers do not, and a statement that a company has a x\% chance of insolvency is false until we correct it by
$\text{odds}_{\text{true}} = r \cdot \text{odds}_{\text{sampled}}$.

\subsection{Verification}
Finally, we performed three checks to determine if our design choiches where corrected:
\begin{itemize}
    \item First, we tested leakage. Structurally the boundary holds by construction, but structure is an argument rather than evidence, so we measured the correlation of every feature with every label. The strongest is 0.37, and the ordering is economically sensible, with larger companies, companies already carrying debt and companies that have recently taken on charges being the most likely to take on another. A near-unit correlation would have meant a feature was secretly the answer, but this is not the case for our setup.
    \item Second, we stress-tested the June 2025 hole on the label side to determine if the pipeline correctly calculated the April label using the months that actaully exists, withouth accidentally pulling August or declaring the label mission. The April 2025 origin has a three-month window
covering May, June and July, and it must still produce a normal label built from May and July rather than being silently NULL or silently reaching into August. It does.
\item Finally, we tested for base-rate stability. All four rates are flat across all eleven origins and fall inside the plausibility ranges written down before measuring. That is what licenses the out-of-time split, because with no drift in the underlying rate, if the model performs better on the test set than expected, we have more reason to believe that the improvement comes from the model's ability to predict the event, rather than simply because the test period happens to have a very different event rate.
\end{itemize}

A fifth label, \texttt{switching}, was also built from the charge data described in \S\ref{sec:charges}, measuring if the company is changing which lender it uses. It is measured and deliberately not trained as it did not meet the pre-specified sample-size threshold for ML training. But in future work we would have attempted to develop this signal further.

\section{The modelling pipeline as software}
\note{Frame this as reproducibility engineering, not as plumbing.
\texttt{RunConfig}, the three-family model registry, the recalibration step, and
a \texttt{record\_run} that refuses to overwrite an existing tag. Staged
execution via a script rather than a notebook, and the honest reason: the
machine kept rebooting under long jobs. The \texttt{src/} module map, one line
each. Point at Appendix~\ref{appx:runs} for the run registry.}

In this section we go through the machinery that made a run cost one line of code.

\subsection{The three axes for a run, model families \& evaluation settings}
Firstly, we need to establish what the three axes are that constitute a run: which matrix is read, which feature list is applied to it and which model families are fitted. In our previous design, each was a separate keyword argument threaded through six functions, which meant they had to agree else the run would have silently done the wrong thing. This design caused a silent failure, for instance pointing at the wider "lender matrix" (i.e. the one including additional variables on lender data) while passing the baseline feature list simply dropped the lender columns and raised nothing at all. To fix this crippled design, we tied the three azes into one frozen \texttt{RunConfig}, which makes the illegal combinations unpresentable and makes a run reproducible from a single value. 

We applied these changes after a supervisor review on the 27th July 2026, after which we turned the two hard-coded fitting functions called by name inside the training loop into a registry in which each entry knows how to build a complete model architecture, whether it requires the dense preprocessing path (i.e. special preprocessing used for dense models), which SHAP explainer applies to it, and what its hyperparameter grid is. The training loop iterates the registry. This, in turns allows us to add a new model family by simply adding one dictionary entry, which is what made fitting a third family affordable at all.

We use the three model families according to this rationale:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gradient-boosted trees} \cite{ke2017lightgbm} are the real model.
    \item \textbf{Regularised logistic regression} is the interpretable floor, meaning that if the boosting cannot clearly beat a linear model on the same features, then it is not earning its complexity and we should flag that. Moreover, its signed coefficients serve as an independent check on the SHAP directions, since a disagreement means one of the two its wrong.
    \item The \textbf{multi-layer perceptron} occupies the complex end of the interpretability trade-off; its job is not to win. 
\end{itemize}

Both dense families share a single preprocessing block of imputation, scaling and one-hot encoding\footnote{Imputation replaces missing feature values with estimated values based on the observed data; scaling transforms numerical features to a comparable scale, commonly by centring them at zero and dividing by their standard deviation; and one-hot encoding represents a categorical variable as a set of binary indicator variables, one for each category. These transformations are standard preprocessing steps for machine-learning estimators. \cite{scikit_learn_preprocessing}}. This is deliberate, because if the network and the linear floor saw differently prepared features, the comparison between them would be about the preprocessing rather than about the model.

During imputation, missingness is flagged rather than filled, via an imputer configured to add an indicator column. A NULL delta means "cannot say", and that is information the boosted model expresses natively, and the dense models cannot express at all without the indicator. The behaviour of that same imputer on a column with no observed value in training turns out to matter a great deal, and is taken up in \S\ref{sec:limits}.

Additionally, we implemented three smaller decisions to protect the evaluation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Early stopping for performance validation runs on a company-grouped slice of the training rows and never on the test set, using the test set for early stopping would use the evaluation data to make a training decision, compromising the independence of the final performance estimate; scikit-learn's network does its own early stopping on a random slice \cite{scikit-learn_early_stopping_gradient_boosting} and offers no way to supply a grouped one, this may cause early-stopping to train somewhat longer than ideal, but the final out-of-time test set remains completely independent and is used only after training is finished.
    \item The dense models are row-capped at 500,000 and 300,000 rows respectively; this means we are limiting how many observations are fed into the dense models, not how many features. We do this because backpropagation over a dense one-hot matrix is by far the slowest operation in the module, and what we want from it is a fair comparison rather than the best obtainable network. 
    \item Finally, the evaluation matrices are rebuilt with no downsampling at all, because precision@$N$ does not survive negative downsampling and it is the headline metric we use in the dashboard. We use the same matrix-building function for both purposes, with the negative keep rate set below 1 for training and to 1 for evaluation.
\end{itemize} 

To tune, we could not rely on the default $k$-fold split, because that would reintroduce inside the search exactly the two leaks the primary split exists to prevent: the reported validation score would rise while the out-of-time score did not move. We therefore built a splitter that applies the origin and embargo rule of \S\ref{sec:train-test-split-logic} recursively inside the training origins and never touches the test origins. The cost is that the number of folds becomes a maximum rather than a guarantee. That cost is larger than we expected, and it is measurable. Across the eleven recorded runs there are thirty-two completed tuning jobs on disk, on legal folds, recorded in each run's manifest; re-running the search across all four targets gives the amount of independent evidence each one actually holds:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
target & horizon & train origins & legal folds & boosted CV spread \\
\midrule
lending & 3m & 5 & 3 & 0.0221 \\
insolvency & 6m & 3 & 1 & 0.0359 \\
voluntary exit & 6m & 3 & 1 & 0.0230 \\
growth & 12m & 3 & 0 & not tunable \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Growth cannot be tuned at all. A twelve-month embargo across three training origins leaves no validation origin with anything legal to train on, so the splitter returns nothing and the search refuses rather than returning a degenerate answer. A standard $k$-fold would have manufactured five folds for a target that holds no independent evidence whatsoever. Insolvency and voluntary exit return one fold each, where lending returns three. This is the splitter's honesty priced out: on one target of four, the price is total. We measured what adopting the search winner would do rather than assuming it. On lending, the target with the most folds, refitting LightGBM at the winning configuration and pairing it against the shipped default over 2,782,591 held-out rows moves ROC-AUC by $-0.0008$, with a paired interval of $[-0.0013, -0.0003]$ that excludes zero on the negative side. Precision@$500$ moves $+0.018$ with a lower bound of exactly $+0.000$, which \S\ref{sec:errors} disqualifies on its own rule. Adopting the tuned parameters would have made the shipped model very slightly worse. The reason is worth stating, because it is a property of the search rather than of the model. RandomizedSearchCV cannot be handed an evaluation set, so every configuration is scored at a fixed 1,500 boosting rounds. At 1,500 rounds a slow, narrow configuration is still improving while a fast, wide one overfitted long ago, so what the search ranks is how gracefully a configuration tolerates being run far past its useful length. The production path never goes there: under company-grouped early stopping the shipped default stops at 143 rounds and the tuned winner at 262. This is why the search picks the same corner of the grid on all three tunable targets, 31 leaves at learning rate 0.03 with a minimum of 100 child samples, and ranks the shipped default tenth of twelve every time. That consistency looks like a strong signal and is an artefact of the fixed round count. The search and the deployed model are not optimising the same object. The parameters were therefore never fed back into a fit. Every recorded run sits at the same fixed boosting parameters, and each tuning payload records this as applied: false.

The pipeline also verifies the recalibration of the downsampling. As mentioned, downsampling negatives at rate $r$ multiplies the odds by $1/r$, so we implement a correction: one line applied to every predicted probability, using the keep rate that \S\ref{sec:targets} deliberately carried through on every row. The check is that the mean recalibrated prediction lands on the true base rate of the test population, which is the cheapest available test that the correction is the right way round and would be an easy thing to get backwards without noticing. 

Finally, we also need to explain the models. We programmed the pipeline so that it automatically chooses an appropriate explanation method for each one:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The dispatcher routes the boosted model (LightGBM) to \texttt{TreeExplainer}, which is exact, but it does so on a stratified subsample of 100,000 test rows; stratification matters here, since a plain draw at a base rate of 0.33\% holds around 330 positives and the resulting summary plot would describe the negatives almost exclusively.
    \item Logistic regression is read through its coefficients rather than explained, for the reason given in \S2.3.
    \item The neural network is much harder to explain, so we use the more general KernelExplainer, but only on 20 observations because it is computationally expensive. The measured costs are in \S\ref{sec:results}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Recording a run}
Every run owns a directory holding a manifest (.json), its metrics (.json), and one SHAP importance table (.csv) per target, with a regenerated index across all runs. The manifest is the part that matters: it records the matrix directory, the evaluation directory, the contract and lender sources, the exact feature list together with a short hash of it, the categoricals, the targets, the model set, the registry as it stood, the boosting parameters, the git commit and the timestamp. A run is reproducible from its manifest without reading any code, and two runs are compared by diffing two manifests rather than by remembering what was done. We also deliberately programmed the writer so that it refuses to overwrite an existing tag: the baseline run is the fixed reference the whole A/B design depends on, and under the previous flat layout re-running a single cell would have silently destroyed it. We also have a index containing all our runs, which is regenerated from the directories on every write, so the comparison table cannot drift from what is actually on disk. 
Execution is staged through a script rather than a Jupyter notebook, and the reason is a technical limitation rather than an architectural one: the machine kept rebooting under long jobs. A run therefore had to be resumable and had to survive losing its terminal. The same principle was used for the 25-hour Charges data harvest of \S\ref{sec:charges} recoverable from its own output rather than from a side ledger. 
Finally, we found a WSL2-specific performance problem: asking LightGBM to use all 22 logical cores caused severe thread contention. Restricting it to eight cores made the same computation dramatically faster. Fifty boosting rounds on the 165,000-row insolvency matrix took 98 seconds with unlimited threads and 0.9 seconds with eight, for an identical result.

The module map is given in Table~\ref{tab:modules} and the full run registry in Appendix~\ref{appx:runs}.

\subsection{Baseline}
The \texttt{baseline} run is the 41-feature control matrix fitted with the boosted and linear families only, recorded before the registry rewrite and before the network existed. It is retained rather than superseded because it is the fixed reference against which the added value of the Charges API harvest is measured, and destroying it would have destroyed that measurement rather than merely inconveniencing us.

It is not, however, a like-for-like row in the results table, and Chapter 4 says so wherever it appears. It holds eight rows rather than twelve because it predates the third model family, and
it predates the bootstrap, so it carries point estimates with no intervals attached. 

After rewriting the pipeline, we ran the same baseline configuration through the new code. The three reported metrics were exactly the same as before: ROC-AUC 0.761520, PR-AUC 0.016923 and precision@$100$ 19.0\%. This confirms that the software rewrite changed the structure of the code without changing the underlying modelling result.



\section{Unstructured data}
\label{sec:unstructured-method}

This section explains the process which we used to collected the sources and are described in \S\ref{sec:public-sources} and connect with the register. This focuses on the methodology, we discuss the results that were produced by each source in \S\ref{sec:sources-eval}, while we anlyse their meaning in \S\ref{sec:unstructured}. We carried out the work from the begining of June until the middle of August 2026.

\subsection{The entity spine}
\label{sec:spine}

The database spine contains one row for each company and stores important identification details including the cleaned registration number, standardized name, previous names, postcode and town. We standardized company names by converting the to uppercase, removing punctuation and removing legal endings as the same company. We format registration numbers to eight characters while keeping letter prefixes unchanged, maeaning \texttt{SC12345} becomes \texttt{SC012345} rather than \texttt{OSC12345}. a separate crosswalk table stores all external identifiers that can be connected to a company, including their identifiers type, source and confidence level. This method allows us to make each decision to match a company once and reuse it consistently.

The initial spine included 869,043 companies and 130,945 previous names records covering 107,776 companies. We tested two external identifier connections: Wikidata provides us with the links to Companies House numbers and GLEIF provides the links for Legal Entity Identifiers. We collected and stored both datasets locally , then matched them exactly where available, producing 855 linked companies in total.

\subsection{Matching, and the confidence ladder}
\label{sec:matching-ladder}

We can match sources that provide a registration number and join them without any additional matching. For sources that do not have registration numbers, we match them using a sequence of incresingly weaker checks. We stop once we reach the first successful match condition:

\begin{enumerate}
\item the normalised company name and full postcode match, and this combination
      identifies only one company;
\item the normalised company name and postcode \emph{area} match, used when the
      source provides only an area;
\item the normalised company name and registered town match.
\end{enumerate}

If a matching key identifies multiple companies, we reject the match instead of making an uncertain choice. Similarly, we remove records without location information rahter than matching them using only the company name. We made these decisions intentionally to reduce coverage in order to improve accuracy. An early contracts test without location verification incorrectly linked an Oxford "Amazon Ltd" to Amazon Web Services contracts and associated "Ace Tax Ltd" with taxi-related work. We consider a wrong company matching to be more harmful than a missing company information. We give each of the successful match a confidence score between 0.80 and 1.00 based on the matching level used and we clearly mark any matches below 1.00 for users.

\subsection{The five collections}

 We collected \textbf{Public Contracts} from Contracts Finder over a rolling time period. We stored the data during the first collection and matched it using supplier numbers whenever available. Where supplier numbers were missing, we matched using the company name and postcode.

We collected \textbf{Job Adverts} from Adzuna but since the API does not support searching directly by employer, our approach was to download the national job feed and match employer names back to companies in the entity spine.

We obtained \textbf{Property} information from the Land Registry commerical dataset containing approximately 3.5 million property titles. We read the data in sections of 250,000 rows to avoid memory issues. We transformed the data into separate rows for each owner before matching as each property title can contain up to four owners. Also all property matches are exact as company registration numbers are included directly in the dataset. Titles without a proprietor date cannot be assigned to a timeline and we store them separately rather than removing them.

We collected \textbf{Trade marks} from the IPO open data release containing 1,188,992 records stored in a UTF-16 encoded, pipe separated file. We performed matching using the normalised company name and postcode area which is the most detailed location information provided by the source.

We collected \textbf{Research Grants} from the UKRI Gateway to Research API, covering 97,102 organisation across 972 pages. We cached each page and designed it to support contnuation if interrupted. We performed matching using the company name and postcode.

\subsection{News, and the sample that was searched}

We collected news data from the Guardian Open Platform over the twelve months ending 31 July 2026. We kept the search only in certain sectors such as business, money, technology and Uk news categories, making one cached request for each company. A previous finding by our teamate searched a balanced sample of 96 companies covering four company size groups across three sectors with eight companies in each group, ensuring that smaller categories still contained enough examples. We repeated the analysis using the companies most relevant for commercial use: the 398 companies ranked highest by the models in July 2026 across the four target areas.

To compare the two studies fairly we use the same methodology. Each search used the cleaned company name as an exact phrase. We only included articles when the company name appeared as a separate phrase in the summary fields rather than only inside the article body. This mention also needs support from the registered town, company sector or relevant busiess context terms. Changing the sample while keeping the method unchanged allows the comparison in \S\ref{sec:unstructured} to be meaningful.

\subsection{Output shape and reproducibilty }

We store all source data using two common formats. The first conatains one row per dated event including the company number, date, event type , readable description, value, source link, confidence score and matching method. The second contains one row per company summarising information such as event counts, earliest and latest dates, recent activity and totals. We manage cleaning and data writing functions with a shared software module, meaning that adding a new source only requires creating an events dataset in the expected format.

We record missing information as zero instead of keeping it empty because a company that was checked and had no records is different from a company that was never investigated in the first place. To make the runs faster, we save every API response during the first collection, this ensures that the results can be reproduced and prevents demonstrations from failing when an external service is temporarily unavailable.

 \subsection{The statutory record reaches them completely, but too late}

 The Gazette shows the opposite behaviour. It records legal notices for insolvency events regardless of company size. However , its coverage is foucsed entirely on companies where the failure event has already occurred. Based on the lifecycle classification, it covers 75 percent of companies that are already Insolvent but only 0.075 percent of companies that are still trading. A source that strogly identifies falied companies but provides almost no information about active companies is useful for confirmation but not for prediction. We should also note that 42.5 percent of insolvency notices do not include a company number. We exlcuded these records rather than matching them using company names, meaning that the reported coverage is likely lower than the actual true total.

\subsection{The synthesis}

News media does not provide meaningful coverage of companies at this size. The stautory record identifes them effectively but only after a serious problem has occurred, which is also too late for a bank seeking early prediction. Neither of the sources provides an effective early warning signal. We measure this negative finding through testing rather than just assuming, and it provides the main reason for using structured company register data as the foundation of the forecasting model instead of relying on text based information.

\section{Engineering practice}

A constant risk in a project like this is that the code used to explore an idea and the code used to finally train the model can slowly drift apart. So that chart in a notebook can no longer be reproduced by the pipeline. Three practical steps was taken to prevent this.

\paragraph{One shared definition of every feature}
Feature or label logic was never re-written in each notebook. It was defined once, in a shared Python package \texttt{(src/features)} and \texttt{(src/models)} and imported by every notebook rather than copied. The panel and its deltas, the contract and lender features, the targets and training pipeline were all defined there and called from the exploratory notebooks. This guarantees that the matrix used for modelling is exactly the same one we explored.

It also makes the logic testtable, the leakage and consistency tests run against the same functions that build the features, rather than against a separate notebook version that might quietly diverge. The modelling pipeline itself was built on scikit-learn \cite{sklearn_docs}, with a small registry so that adding a new model was a small change rather than edit threaded through several functions.

\paragraph{A branch per workstream}
Since five people worked in paralled, version control was organised as one feature branch per workstream rather than a single shared line. Each strand namely filtering, attrition, the structured panel and modelling, the unstructured lab and harmonisation owned its own branch so work processed without people overwriting one another, and the history recorded who did what and when.

\paragraph{Runs that could be re-checked}
Every modelling run writes down exactly how it has been produced. Alongside its metrics, each run wrote its full configuration, which feature matrix was used, the exact ordered list of features and the short \texttt{feature\_hash} , the model settings, the code version and a timestamp into small record on disk and refused to overwrite an existing one. Two things followed. A run could be reproduced from its record without reading any code and two runs could be compared by simply looking at the difference between their records. Any changes in the results is always traceable to change in inputs, not to an untracked edit.

\subsection{Evaluation design}
\label{sec:evaldesign}
\note{State the rules before the numbers, so no result later looks chosen after
the fact. Why ranking rather than classification. Why ROC-AUC and precision@$N$
together, and what each one actually answers: ROC-AUC is a global ranking score
that survives the negative downsampling; precision@$N$ is what a relationship
manager experiences. Why per-origin rather than pooled, which \S\ref{sec:errors}
then justifies with a measurement. What the bootstrap intervals resample.}

Before we delve into the resulting number we obtained we need to explain the metrics we picked and why we chose those. As established in \S\ref{sec:modelling-backgroud}, the final task of our deliverable is ranking rather than classification, so the evaluation is one of ordering. For this reason, two metrics are reported for every model on every target, because they answer different questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item ROC-AUC measures how well the model ranks positive cases above negative cases across the whole population. It has the useful property that it survives the 10:1 negative downsampling of \S\ref{sec:targets} in expectation, since dropping a uniform random subset of negatives does not change pairwise ordering. 
    \item Precision@$N$ is different. It looks only at the top of the ranking, for example the top 100 or top 500 companies. This is much closer to what a relationship manager actually experiences. However, precision@N is strongly affected by downsampling.
\end{itemize}

Every test origin is therefore rebuilt with no downsampling before anything is measured, using
the same matrix builder with the keep rate capped at one.

All operational figures are reported per origin rather than pooled. Section \S\ref{sec:errors} explains why this is necessary.

Every metric carries a 95\% confidence interval obtained by resampling the evaluation rows with replacement, one thousand replicates, percentile method. These confidence intervals should be interpreted carefully. They measure uncertainty caused by which companies happened to be in the evaluation sample. They do not include uncertainty from choosing different training periods or from fitting the model again with a different random seed. They are therefore a lower bound on total uncertainty. When comparing two models evaluated on exactly the same companies at the same origins, we use a paired bootstrap: both models are evaluated on the same bootstrap sample, so the variation that is common to both models cancels out. This gives a more precise estimate of the difference between the models. \S\ref{sec:errors} shows that the unpaired reading is inconclusive on the comparison that matters most, where the paired reading is not.

Finally, six of the eleven recorded runs are directly comparable to one another and carry bootstrap intervals on all twelve cells. The remaining five are retained as diagnostic rather than comparative: three are superseded by deterministic re-recordings that reproduce them exactly, and two are kept as exhibits of the defect described in §\ref{sec:leak}, where an interval on a number we are presenting as broken would serve no purpose. The body therefore tabulates the six comparable runs; all eleven appear in Appendix~\ref{appx:runs}.

    \item Voluntary exit is strongly related to filing behaviour. Here we flag a methodological correction when interpreting SHAP. As mentioned, we validate SHAP directions against the signed coefficients of the linear model, on the principle that where the two disagree one of them is wrong. Hence, the average signed SHAP value is not a measure of direction. A feature can have different effects at different values. Therfore, direction is measured using the Spearman correlation between the feature value and its own SHAP value. This does not assume that the relationship is linear.

Most features give similar directions in the SHAP analysis and the logistic model. The main disagreements occur for insolvency. This is not necessarily a problem because logistic coefficients measure a feature's effect while holding the other variables constant, while SHAP shows the feature's relationship with predictions. Hence, when features are strongly correlated, a partial effect can honestly flip sign against the marginal relationship SHAP displays.

\subsection{Is the target balanced, and what does that do to the metrics?}
The four targets we are aiming to predict have very different base rates. They range from 0.26\% for lending to about 8\% for voluntary exit. This matters because the same predicted metric can mean very different things based on the target. For instance, if a model predicts "no" for every company on the lending task, it would achieve 99.74\% accuracy. On the surface, that sounds excellent, but the model would be misleading given that its predicting accuracy on a very low base rate. The same problem applies to recall, precision and F1 performance metrics. Instead, we use ranking metrics such as ROC-AUC and precision@$N$. We also report a column \texttt{lift}, defined as precision@$N$ divided by the target's base rate. This is the only fair way to compare a result on a target that fires 1:100 with one that fires 1:12.

This imbalance also affects how much downsampling we need:
\begin{itemize}
    \item For insolvency, the dataset is reduced by about 27 times, from 13.8 million rows to 520,000, without removing any positive cases.
    \item Lending is reduced by about 33 times.
    \item Voluntary exit is different: the dataset only falls from 13.8 million to 11.9 million rows, with a keep rate of 0.85. This happens because voluntary exit has a much higher base rate of about 7.8\%, so there is little need to remove additional negative observations.
\end{itemize}

The number of positive cases also determines how precise the confidence intervals can be:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Voluntary exit has about 215,000 positive cases, so it has the narrowest intervals, around ±0.001.
    \item Lending has only about 7,500 positive cases, so its intervals are much wider, around ±0.0045.
\end{itemize}

We must also interpret with caution the results of voluntary exit. Its apparently strong precision@$N$ is partly caused by many companies receiving exactly the same score. Therefore, precision@$N$ is partly measuring which companies were selected from a large tied group, rather than measuring a precise ranking, for reasons measured in \S\ref{sec:errors}, and it should not be read to three digits.

\subsubsection{The symptom}
Since we already started with a pretty solid model, including many deltas, the original comparison was designed to test if the 25 hours of Charges API harvest improved prediction. 

For three of the four targets, the answer appeared to be no. However, the lending result looked extremely strong: precision@$100$ of 1.000 against a base rate of 0.271\%, and
precision@$500$ of 0.992, a lift of 366 times, while ROC-AUC moved only from 0.8767 to 0.8951.

These two things cannot co-exist, and it's the reason why \S\ref{sec:evaldesign} reports both metrics. If the model had generally become much better at ranking companies, both ROC-AUC and precision@$N$ should have improved substantially. Instead, precision@$N$ became almost perfect, while ROC-AUC changed only slightly. 

The explanation is that ROC-AUC looks at th,e ranking across about 2.8 million evaluation rows, while precision@$N$ looks only at the very top of the list. Hence, if the model receives information about the outcome for only a small group of companies, that can make the top of the list look almost perfect, while having very little effect on the global ranking. The gap between a global metric and a local one is the entire tell, and had we tracked only AUC we would have shipped this.

Before explaining this bizarreness with leakage, we ruled out several simpler explanations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The test rows and the positive counts were identical between the runs, so the comparison was fair.
    \item The lender features existed for 100\% of the rows because we designed for companies without charges to be assigned assigned 0 rather than a NULL. Hence, this rules out any selection effect.
    \item Finally, the best individual feature had a precision@$500$ of only 0.164, while the full model reached 0.992. This meant that no individual column was driving the label. On the contrary, the model was combining several features, which demanded further analysis rather than isolating one single problematic feature.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{The cause}
Every charge in the Companies House API carries: \texttt{created\_on}, the date the charge was made; \texttt{delivered\_on}, the date it reached the registrar and became visible to anyone looking.

The original as-of join used the first one and this created the leakage.

Imagine that a charge was created on 20 January, but delivered on 28 January. The original system could include this charge in the feature row for 1 January, even though the charge was not yet visible at that time. 

The lending label, however, was calculated from the bulk register. That register could not contain the charge before it had been delivered. The feature, therefore, contained information that the label could only know later. 

The model was not actually predicting future lending. Instead, it was partly reading information about something that had already happened.

We also measured this disruption, making the size of the problem even clearer. 

For companies that went on to borrow within three months, the API's charge count was already 0.29 charges higher than the register's count. In 19.2\% of these cases, the API had already reached the future post-borrowing value. 

Across the whole population that same gap is about 0.05, so the discrepancy is six times larger precisely where it does damage.

If we then consider the top 500 companies ranked by the model, 46.6\% had a charge that was counted before it was observable. Across the whole population, this happened for only 0.01\% of companies. This was the strongest evidence of leakage.

\subsubsection{The obvious fix we attempted}

The first obvious solution we attempted to fix the problem was to use \texttt{delivered\_on} instead of \texttt{created\_on}. This reduced the problem substantially, moving the mean gap from 0.29 to 0.16 and the share of companies already at the future value from 19.2\% to 9.7\%, but the problem was not gone altogether. After rerunning the model, lending precision@$500$ was still 0.742, compared with 0.282 for the control model.

After digging deeper into how the charge API works, we discovered that delivery is not the same as registration: after a charge is delivered, the registrar still needs time to process it before it appears in the register used to compute the label. To estimate that delay, we ran a sweep: increasing a margin day by day until the API's charge count agreed on average with the register's count. Three choices went into how we ran it, each a sensible-looking default at the time. We swept over only seven monthly origins rather than all of them, to limit the cost of repeatedly rebuilding and comparing the panel. We assumed each register extract was cut on the 1st of the month, which is what the monthly bulk file is nominally dated and is true the large majority of the time. And we measured the gap only on companies that go on to borrow (the subset where the distortion is worst) on the instinct of calibrating where the problem actually shows up. Comparing the two counts this way, the margin that made them agree on average came out at 21 days, and that is the figure we fitted.

\subsubsection{The calibration}
The 21-day value initially looked reasonable, but we decomposed it. Walking from the old estimate to the new one, changing one thing at a time with the same estimator throughout, we created an audit trail that helped us in our calibration. The aim was to figure out how many days of "margin" to add so that the API's view of a company's charges lined up with the register's view. We did so by sweeping the margin until the two counts agreed on average. The 21-day answer came out of one particular way of running that sweep, and this decomposition re-runs it correctly, one change at a time, to see where the 21 came from:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Using all origins rather than seven moves the crossing from 22.1 to 23.7 days
    \item Using the real extract date moves it from 23.7 to 23.6
    \item Pointing the sweep at the right target moves it from 23.6 to 5.3: the register count it had been matching against moves both when a charge is created and when one is satisfied, so calibrating the creation clock against it forced the margin to absorb the satisfaction (i.e. a charge exiting when repaid) clock's error too; matching against a count that reflects creation alone removes that.
    \item and finally, removing the conditioning on companies that go on to borrow moves it from 5.3 to 1.8.
\end{itemize}

The key result is that 18 of the 21 days came from the satisfaction clock. 

 There was also independent evidence of the leakage inside the model itself. In fact, under the leaking \texttt{created\_on} gate, \texttt{n\_of\_charges\_outstanding} was the most important feature, with a mean absolute SHAP value of 0.414, ranking 1st out of 54 features. After we changed to \texttt{delivered\_on}, it fell to 0.284, ranking 3rd. And after we added the 21-day margin, it fell almost completely to 0.002, ranking 40th. A similar pattern appeared for \texttt{lbg\_share\_of\_outstanding}, whose importance fell from 0.146 to 0.073 to 0.002. 
 
This mattered because it is not derived from the metric we are trying to explain. precision@$100$ = 1.000 tells
us the top of the list is wrong. Thanks to the SHAP ranking, we could understand which feature the model was using to obtain that wrong information. Two independent instruments, one mechanism.

Moving on to other results we obtained from the decomposition, we originally thought that most of the 21 days were caused by differences in the monthly extract dates. In reality, we showed this explains only 0.11 days. The extract date usually was the first of the month: 29 of 33 extracts were on the first, with an average drift of 0.39 days. The correction actually goes in the opposite direction from what we expected because an extract taken after the first includes more charges, not fewer. So extract drift is real, but it is not the main explanation.

The deeper issue is that the register is only observed once a month. If a charge is delivered on the tenth, it cannot appear in the observed register until the next monthly extract, regardless of how quickly the registrar processes it. Therefore, matching the average API count to the monthly register can incorrectly interpret waiting time as processing time.

\subsubsection{The final solution}
Instead of a mean-matching sweep, we calibrated on first appearance. For each charge, we find the first monthly extract in which the register shows that charge. This gives\footnote{The confidence intervals collapse to a single point because the estimated value is stable, not because the underlying criterion is beyond debate.}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item creation lag: 4 days (95\% CI [4, 4], (n=51,669))
    \item satisfaction lag: 1 day (95\% CI [1, 1], (n=47,480))
\end{itemize}

This was the best overall solution.

Moreover, the old 21-day gate carried a large practical cost. The margin exists to prevent leakage, but pushing it too far causes the opposite problem: charges that the register already contained, and that the model was entitled to see, get withheld from the feature row anyway, leaving the model to work from an outdated picture. This is staleness, and at 21 days it was severe. The gate bought only 0.60 percentage points of reduced leakage while paying 43 percentage points of staleness for it, so that more than half of all charges were hidden for a full month after they had genuinely become visible. That is why the 21-day model scored below the plain control: it was no longer peeking at future charges, but it was now blind to past ones it should have used.

But then, why did the original test never caught it?
The original verification test truncated the charge history at a cut-off date and checked whether the rebuilt panel matched the original panel. However, both the truncation and the panel used \texttt{created\_on}. Same clock on both sides, so the test could never fail. In other words, the test was effectively asking if the panel included charges created after the cut-off date. By construction, the answer was no. Instead, we were trying to verify if the panel included charges that were already created but were not yet observable at the cut-off date.

The rewritten test keeps the reference clock and the visibility clock separate. It also checks that rebuilding the panel from the full history reproduces the stored panel. Any difference in the blind replay can therefore be attributed to the truncated history rather than to the test itself.

There is one more important lesson.

Even the rewritten test would have passed if it had been run on only one month. Hence, we tested it on:
\begin{itemize}
    \item January 2025, with no extract drift
    \item February 2024, with the largest drift of six days
\end{itemize}

In February 2024, the six-day extract drift happened to compensate for the missing satisfaction delay. The leakage therefore remained hidden.

From this we learnt that a blind-replay test (i.e. taking the full history, cutting it off at some date, rebuilding what the model should have been able to see at that date, and checking it matches the stored data) is successful contingent on having the same date field for when you cut and when you check against and the range of months you run it on.

The final table and figures document the four gates and their sensitivity range. The main lending precision@$500$ sequence is:

0.282 → 0.992 → 0.742 → 0.310 → 0.332

with a sensitivity band from 0.294 to 0.406.

\subsection{Does counterparty identity help? (N4)}
\note{The verdict, read off the most conservative gate against the deterministic
control, and the reasoning that makes it more than a null. The apparent gain
scales monotonically with how much margin the gate allows, in every model family;
one model getting greedier is a story about that model, three families moving in
lockstep is a story about the data. The mechanism sentence the section turns on:
the lending label is computed from the very register the gate governs, so
loosening visibility leaks the label directly, whereas insolvency comes from a
separate register and shows no gradient at all under the same loosening. Two
targets, opposite behaviour, one mechanism, and we did not design that natural
experiment. Then the answer with its caveats: at the tightest gate tested, the
lender features give a boosted model nothing and give the linear model a small
but real gain, which locates the finding in \emph{encoding} rather than in
information, because the base feature set already carries mortgage-charge counts.
Both caveats travel with it: the tightest gate tested is not a gate proved
clean, and the most impressive precision number the project produced is one we
are throwing away on purpose.}

Once again, the whole point of the test was to see if adding features from the Charger API would increase model accuracy against the 41-feature control model. Across the four targets and the three model families, here we present the most conservative result, applying the solution to the leakage explained in \S\ref{sec:leak}.

The strongest evidence comes from the pattern across the gate settings rather than from any one number. As we showed in previous section, the apparent improvement increases monotonically as the visibility gate becomes more permissive. This happens in all three model families and in all nine relevant cells. This is important, If we observed only one model improving as the gate loosened, that would be a model-specific improvement. However, since all three families show the same pattern, it is much more likely that the pattern comes from the data itself.

The reason becomes clear when comparing lending with insolvency. The lending label is calculated from the same charge register controlled by the visibility gate. If the gate is loosened, the model gets earlier access to information that directly contributes to its own label. That creates leakage. On the other hand, the insolvency label comes from a completely different register. The gate affects the features but has no path to the insolvency label.

Therefore, insolvency acts as a control experiment. If the lender features were simply useful information, both lending and insolvency should improve in a similar way regardless of the gate. If the gate is leaking the lending label, only lending should respond strongly to the gate. That is what we observe.

For lending, precision@$500$ is 0.294, 0.332 and 0.406 across the three gate settings against a control of 0.282, with the two looser gates clear of zero on the paired interval. For insolvency, there is no similar pattern. The linear model stays around +0.0023 at all three settings. The insolvency MLP does increase across the ladder, but the change is negative and much smaller than its seed variation. It therefore does not provide useful evidence. Removing those MLP rows makes the main argument stronger because the monotonic pattern remains in the two model families whose results are reproducible enough to interpret.

\subsubsection{Verdict by model family: LightGBM}

The boosted model gains essentially nothing from the thirteen additional lender features.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Across the four targets, its largest absolute ROC-AUC change is only 0.0007.
    \item For voluntary exit and growth, the paired intervals actually show small negative changes of -0.0002 and -0.0005.
\end{itemize}

This means that the result is not simply that we did not find evidence of an improvement. More importantly, the evidence shows that the boosted model does not benefit from these additional features and may be very slightly worse.

After 25 hours of data harvesting, the boosted model is already extracting the useful information from the mortgage-charge count features in the original feature set.

\subsubsection{Verdict by model family: Logistic}
The linear model is different, and we measured all four of its deltas on a paired bootstrap, for the reason set out in \S\ref{sec:errors}. Refitting logistic under the control and under the tightest gate in a single process, aligning the evaluation rows on origin month and company number, and resampling both models on the same replicate gives:

\subsubsection{Verdict by model family: MLP}
The neural network is not interpreted because its seed instability is too large.

\subsubsection{Ineffectiveness of some lenders metrics}
There is also an internal model check supporting the conclusion. In the \texttt{lender\_fixed} LightGBM model, \texttt{is\_lbg\_client} has a mean absolute SHAP value of exactly 0.000000 on all four targets. It ranks between 51st and 53rd out of 54 features. This means that LightGBM never used this feature in a split across its 1,500 trees.

Before interpreting that as evidence, we checked whether the model was actually capable of using the feature: with \texttt{min\_child\_samples} = 200, a binary feature needs at least 200 positive examples to be usable in a split. \texttt{is\_lbg\_client} has between 5,085 and 103,182 positive cases depending on the target, so it easily passes this requirement.

Its zero importance therefore means the model chose not to use the feature.

By contrast, \texttt{competitor\_entered\_12m} also has zero importance, but it does not have enough positive cases for the model to use it reliably on some targets. Its zero importance is therefore a limitation of model capacity, not evidence that the feature is useless.

\texttt{ever\_lbg\_client} and \texttt{lbg\_charge\_satisfied\_6m} are also barely used, with mean absolute SHAP values never above ($3.1\times10^{-4}$).

\subsubsection{Conclusion}
The broader conclusion is more useful than simply saying that the lender features do not help. The additional columns mostly provide a different encoding of information that the base features already contain. Hence, while the boosted model can already extract this information from the raw charge counts, the linear model cannot easily create the interactions needed to do so, so it benefits from the richer encoding.

Therefore, the finding is mainly about encoding rather than new information. Voluntary exit is the exception that keeps the reading honest. There, the linear model is measurably slightly worse with the lender columns, by $-0.0010$ with an interval that excludes zero, which is the same shape as the boosted verdict on voluntary exit and growth. So the claim is not that the richer encoding helps the linear model everywhere. It is that where the base features already carry the charge counts in a form the model can use, the extra columns are at best redundant and can cost a little.
This means the data harvest could still be useful when interpretability is important and a linear model is being used. However, tt is not useful for the boosted model that we actually deploy.

Two caveats are important.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item First, the tightest gate we tested has not been proven completely clean. The lending performance is still increasing at that gate. We therefore know that the remaining lookahead is smaller than at the calibrated gate, but we do not know that it is zero.
    \item Second, the strongest result in the entire project is deliberately being discarded. The loosest gate produces lending precision@$500$ of 0.406, which is a 44\% improvement over the control and a lift of roughly 150 times the base rate. However, this is also the gate with the most leakage. Reporting 0.406 as a genuine model improvement would therefore mean reporting a result that is partly caused by the model seeing its own label.
\end{enumerate}

The number is kept in the report so that anyone who finds it elsewhere also sees the explanation for why it is not used.

\subsection{What the model learned: global SHAP analysis}
\label{sec:label-shap-importance}
We created four beeswarm summaries from the control run, one per target at figures~\ref{fig:beeswarm}. This section interprets those graphs and explains what the models actually learned from the data.

There is one important technical point about all SHAP values in this chapter. \texttt{TreeExplainer} for \texttt{LGBMClassifier} gives contributions in log-odds, not probabilities. These contributions add up to the logit of the model's uncalibrated score. Hence, the final score used in production is recalibrated using: $$odds_{true} = odds_{sampled} \times neg\_keep\_rate$$. For example, the top lending company in the July 2026 list has an uncalibrated probability of 0.972 but a final shipped score of 0.500. The ranking does not change because the recalibration is monotonic. Therefore, the SHAP values explain the ranking, while the displayed probability is the recalibrated version of that ranking. The SHAP values should therefore not be added together and expected to reproduce the probability shown on the shortlist.

\subsubsection{Lending}
Lending is mainly a story about company size and existing debt. \texttt{Mortgages.NumMortCharges} is the most important feature, with a mean absolute SHAP value of 0.57. \texttt{segment} follows at 0.46. Companies with no existing charges tend to receive negative SHAP contributions, while companies with many charges tend to receive positive contributions.

This is reasonable for a lending-readiness model, but it also gives a slightly uncomfortable conclusion: the strongest signal for who is likely to borrow next is who has borrowed before. Because of this, a simple top-N ranking will naturally favour larger, highly leveraged companies. An SME team may therefore need to rank companies within size groups rather than across the whole population.

\subsubsection{Voluntary exit}

Voluntary exit is mainly a filing-behaviour story.

The three strongest features are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{months\_since\_last\_confstmt}: 0.38
    \item \texttt{accounts\_stale\_streak\_months}: 0.31
    \item \texttt{days\_to\_next\_accounts\_due}: 0.30
\end{itemize}

These features are interesting because their interpretation changes depending on when they are measured. In a cross-sectional model, they would be leakage. A company that has already stopped filing is already showing the outcome we are trying to predict. But in this project, the features are measured at time (t) and used to predict a strike-off proposal between (t+1) and (t+6). In that setting, the same features become early warning signals.

This is one of the clearest benefits of using a time series: information that would be leakage in a cross-sectional model can become valid predictive information when it is measured before the outcome occurs.

\subsubsection{Insolvency}

Insolvency does not have one dominant feature. \texttt{segment} is the strongest feature, with mean absolute SHAP of 0.73, followed by \texttt{company\_age\_years} at 0.32. After that, importance is spread across age and filing-recency variables. This is consistent with the lower ROC-AUC seen for insolvency. The model is combining many relatively weak signals rather than relying on one powerful predictor. The disadvantage is that there is no simple explanation that can easily be given to a relationship manager for why a particular company appears high in the ranking.

\subsubsection{Growth}
Growth is dominated by \texttt{tier\_rank}, with a mean absolute SHAP value of 0.94, almost twice that of the next feature. This result should be treated carefully. \texttt{tier\_rank} comes from \texttt{Accounts.AccountCategory}, so the model is strongly using the company's current position in the size hierarchy to predict whether it will move to a higher tier. Part of this predictive power is a ceiling effect. A company that is already at the highest tier cannot move to a higher tier. The model may therefore be learning this boundary rather than learning a real mechanism of growth.

This is the weakest of the four SHAP interpretations and should be investigated further.

\subsubsection{Checking the direction of features}

We then compared the direction of the most important features with the signs of the corresponding logistic coefficients. However, there is an important correction to the way direction should be calculated: the average signed SHAP value is not a measure of direction. A feature can have a positive effect for some companies and a negative effect for others. The average can therefore be close to zero or even have the opposite sign from the actual relationship. Instead, we measure direction using the Spearman correlation between the feature's value and its own SHAP value. Using this method, the SHAP and logistic models agree on:
\begin{itemize}
    \item 10/10 top features for growth
    \item 9/10 for voluntary exit
    \item 8/10 for lending
    \item only 3/10 for insolvency
\end{itemize}

The insolvency disagreement is scritically not matching. One possible explanation is that insolvency features have genuinely non-linear relationships, while logistic regression is trying to represent them using straight lines. However, this has not been demonstrated, so it remains a hypothesis.

\subsection{What our error bars are worth}
\label{sec:errors}
\note{Present this as a result, not a footnote, because it is one. Two findings.
(i) We validated our confidence intervals against a second, independent source of
variation, refitting under different seeds, and they held for LightGBM and
logistic and failed for the MLP, where a seed term dominates. In one case two
non-overlapping intervals were reassuring us about a difference whose sign flips
across seeds. State the resulting rule: no MLP difference in this project below
about 0.02 ROC-AUC is interpretable. (ii) The paired bootstrap: comparing two
runs by separate intervals is the wrong test when both are evaluated on the same
companies, and resampling once and differencing tightens the lending interval
about five-fold and moves it from inconclusive to clearly excluding zero.
(iii) Pooled precision@$N$ is not an average of its months; it lands outside the
range of the two months it pools in 9 of 12 rows, in both directions, and the
worst row lands 0.108 below the worse of its own months. So per-origin figures
are the honest ones, and it is also what a relationship manager receives, one
month at a time.}

In this section we explore confidence intervals and the uncertainty analysis results, because they changed which conclusions the project could safely make.

\subsubsection{Bootstrap uncertainty versus model instability}

The bootstrap resamples the evaluation companies. It therefore answers how much does the score change depending on which companies happen to be in the test sample. It does not answer what happens if we fit the model again. These are different sources of uncertainty.

When the growth models were fitted using four different random seeds:
\begin{itemize}
    \item LightGBM varied by 0.0013
    \item Logistic regression varied by 0.0021
\end{itemize}

These values are relatively small compared with their bootstrap intervals. For these two models, the row bootstrap therefore captures most of the relevant uncertainty.

The neural network behaves very differently. Its variation is:

\begin{itemize}
    \item 0.018 for the 41-feature model
    \item 0.031 for the corrected 27-feature model
\end{itemize}


These are four to seven times larger than the bootstrap widths. 


In the light of the results, the practical rule we used throughout the chapter is: for the neural network, a ROC-AUC difference below about 0.02 should not be interpreted as meaningful. A difference between two individual fits carries uncertainty of roughly ±0.035.

The growth feature correction showed why this matters. Initially, it appeared that the correction improved the MLP by +0.0075, with non-overlapping bootstrap intervals.

But when both feature sets were fitted using four different seeds, the paired differences were:
\begin{itemize}
    \item +0.0075
    \item +0.0283
    \item -0.0063
    \item -0.0101
\end{itemize}

The sign therefore changed in two of the four runs. The mean difference was only +0.0048, with a standard deviation of 0.0174. Even worse, the recorded 41-feature model was exactly the worst of the four seeds. The apparent improvement was therefore partly caused by which particular seed happened to be used.

The lesson we learnt is that two non-overlapping bootstrap intervals can still give a misleading impression of a difference if the model itself is unstable across seeds. When the same model is fitted twice in the same process with the same inputs, all three model families produce bit-identical predictions. The issue is fit instability across random seeds: the model is reproducible when the seed is fixed, but different seeds produce genuinely different fitted models.

\subsubsection{Numerical reproducibility}
The dense models are exactly reproducible given a threading configuration, and differ between configurations. Refitting one growth cell at one, four, eight and sixteen BLAS threads gives ROC-AUC 0.738222, 0.738209, 0.738222 and 0.738214, a spread of about $1.3\times10^{-5}$, while two fresh processes at the same thread count agree bit for bit. The residual is therefore deterministic within a configuration rather than random per process. It is irrelevant for ROC-AUC at the scale of any difference this chapter interprets, but it can still matter for precision@$N$, which changes discontinuously when two companies swap positions around the ranking cut-off.

A relative numerical change as small as ($10^{-15}$) can change precision@$500$ by 0.004. For this reason, the report does not treat precision@$N$ differences below about 0.01 as meaningful results.

Voluntary exit provides an extreme example: 982 of the top 1,000 scores are exact ties. This happens because the boosted model assigns the same leaf value to very large groups of companies.

As a result, precision@$N$ is not really measuring a fine ranking among those companies. It partly depends on which companies the partition procedure happens to select from the tied group.

The two rare targets behave differently. Their score gaps around rank 500 are around ($10^{-4}$), so numerical noise is not enough to change their ordering.

\subsubsection{The paired bootstrap}

The paired bootstrap is important when comparing two models. We know that, both models are evaluated on the same companies at the same origins. Therefore, much of the uncertainty caused by the companies themselves is shared by both models. Hence, if we calculate separate confidence intervals for each model and simply ask whether the intervals overlap, we ignore this shared variation. This makes the test unnecessarily conservative. Instead, we resample the rows once and calculate both models' metrics on exactly the same resample. The difference between the two models then becomes the quantity of interest. For the lending comparison, the separate intervals overlap heavily and suggest that the difference is unclear. The paired interval is about five times narrower and clearly excludes zero. The paired method is therefore the appropriate test because the question is not. Instead of asking ourselves if a different group of companies would produce different results, we must investigate if these same companies do receive different rankings under the two models.

\subsubsection{Why pooled precision@$N$ is misleading}
Precision@$N$ calculated by combining several months is not the average of the monthly precision@$N$ values. When the two test origins are pooled together, the pooled value falls outside the range of the two monthly values in 9 of 12 target-and-model-family combinations. In six of those nine cases, the difference is greater than 0.02.

This happens for two reasons.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item For lending, pooling makes the result look better. The pooled precision is 0.282, compared with monthly values of 0.218 and 0.226. This happens because selecting the best 500 companies from two months means selecting less deeply from each individual monthly ranking.
    \item For voluntary exit, pooling makes the result look worse. The pooled value is 0.296, compared with monthly values of 0.404 and 0.450. This can happen because model scores are not necessarily directly comparable across different origins. When the months are pooled, the top 500 can become dominated by whichever month happens to have higher absolute scores, rather than by the companies that are best ranked within their own month.
\end{enumerate}

Therefore, per-origin precision@$N$ is the honest operational metric. It also matches how a relationship manager actually receives the output: one month's ranking at a time.

The pooled metric is kept only when comparing runs in a way that treats both runs identically.

\subsubsection{Final observations}
All of these problems have the same underlying pattern.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A check was run, it produced a clean-looking result, and the result was initially believed.
    \item The pooled number looked like an average.
    \item The tied top-500 looked like a real ranking.
    \item The bootstrap looked like a complete confidence interval.
    \item  The hyperparameter search produced a confident and internally consistent ranking of twelve configurations, identical in its winner across three targets, and that ranking does not survive contact with the model we deploy (\S\ref{sec:pipeline}).
\end{enumerate}

None of these calculations was mathematically wrong. The problem was that we interpreted them as answering questions that they did not actually answer.

The same issue appeared in the blind-replay test in \S\ref{sec:leak}, in the acceptance gate discussed in \S\ref{sec:limits}, and in the hyperparameter search of \S\ref{sec:pipeline}, which scored every candidate at a fixed round count that the production fit never reaches. This is the main process lesson we learned from the modelling: a test is not useful simply because it runs and returns a clean result. We also need to check that the test is capable of detecting the failure it is supposed to detect.

One technical limitation was found late and closed. The staged runner originally persisted only the boosted model, so for most of the project only boosted comparisons could be paired, and the linear verdict of §\ref{sec:n4} rested on unpaired point estimates. The runner now writes the held-out labels, the row keys and one score vector per model family for every target, which costs roughly 30 MB a target and about 120 MB a run rather than the few megabytes we first assumed. Every future comparison between any two runs and any model family is therefore pairable by construction, and the comparison that motivated the change was re-measured directly from the stored matrices, in §\ref{sec:n4}.

\section{The unstructured result: narrative against statutory (N1, N5)}
\label{sec:unstructured}

We tested two types of unstructured text sources using the same question: can public written information reveal anything useful about a small company before a major event occurs? Using the media , one source was narrative-based the other was statutory , using official insolvency records. The two sources show opposite strengths and weaknesses which forms the main conclusion of this analysis.

\subsection{Narrative media does not reach these companies}

Company 16392251, \textsc{Tiger Holdings Ltd}, is a London based company incorporated in April 2025 with no existing field accounts but this was incorrectly linked to an article about the acquisition of Flying Tiger Copenhagen by private equity. The name cleaning process reduces the company name to the word \textsc{Tiger}, which appears separately inside ``Flying Tiger''. The location check also failed because London appears frequently in Guardian business articles. We therefore removed the match rather than counting it as valid.

The other nine false matches had the same pattern. \textsc{Partners and Company Group Ltd} produced 77 articles because the cleaned name became the common phrase ``partners and''. \textsc{Watts Group} was reduced to ``Watts'', and \textsc{Clockwise Group} became ``Clockwise''. These examples show us that common words create significant problems when matching company names to new articles. A Tesco control search using the same period and rules produced 147 raw results with 60 of 50 manually checked articles confirmed as valid , showing that the pipeline can identify news coverage when it actually exists.

We should report two additional points clearly. A previous analysis had showed 95.8 percentage coverage, but this was caused by a reporting error where company results were incorrectly copied across sector groups. We then later indentified and corrected the mistake in the following notebook. We also intentionally keep the verification process strict. For example , \textsc{Jet2 Plc} produced 17 raw articles, but none were actually verified. These articles were likey genuine but because the company name is short single phrase, the matching process cannot rely on additional context words , and articles about the airline may not mention the registered town. Therefore , the accurate conclusion is that verified coverage is zero or very close to zero rather than claiming that it is exactly zero.

\subsection{The statutory record reaches them completely, but too late}

The Gazette shows the opposite behaviour. It records legal notices for insolvency events regardless of company size. A small one-person company receives an official winding up notice in the same way that a large well known company would. This is something news organizations generally do not provide , using the company numbers printed in notices, the Gazette matched 19,814 companies in the dataset, equal to 1.33 per cent of all companies.

However, its coverage is focused entirely on companies where the failure event has already occurred. Based on the lifecycle classification, it covers 75 percent of companies that are already Insolvent but only 0.075 percent of companies that are still trading. A source that strongly identifies falied companies but provides almost no information about active companies is useful for confirmation but not for prediction. We should also note that 42.5 percent of insolvency notices do not include a company number. We excluded these records rather than matching them using company names, meaning that the reported coverage is likey lower than the actual true total.

\subsection{The synthesis}

News media does not accurately provide meaningful coverage of companies at this size. The statutory record indentifies them effectively but only after a serious problem has occurred, which is also too late for a bank seeking early prediction. Neither of the sources provides an effective early warning signal. We measured this negative finding through testing rather than just assuming, and it provides the main reason for using structured company register data as the foundation of the forecasting model instead of relying on text based information.

While in the previous section we evaluated the models, here we're trying to determine the effectiveness of our model for a commercial reader. We are trying to explore, if Lloyds ran this tomorrow, what would land on somebody's desk, and how often would it be right?

So first we need to define what the deliverable actually is: Precision@$N$ is not the shortlist we then use in the deliverable. We use this metric as a report card on a month we held out, describing how many of the model's top $N$ picks went on to do the actual scoring. The list itself comes from the scoring stage: the fitted model is applied to the most recent panel month and writes a recalibrated probability for every one of the 1,409,284 companies active at July 2026, and the shortlists are the top hundred of that ranking, per target. This distinction also answers the natural objection about staleness. The model is trained across thirty-three months but applied to a single current month, which is exactly what a relationship manager would do, so we do not hand out a company that was predicted to fail in October 2023 and has since been struck off; we analyse only the current month's active companies.

Inspecting the per-held-out-month results (rather than pooled, for the reason established in \S\ref{sec:errors}), we can establish how often the model is correct:

The most important metric of the table is lift; voluntary exit is the worked example of why. One might think it looks like the best result with 85\% correct precision. However, it is the weakest of the four, because roughly one company in 12 proposes a strike-off within six months anyway. So, a list picked at random from the register would already be right about 80\% of the time. 

On the other hand, lending readiness at 43\% is the stronger result the project produced on a clean run. This is because it operates at an extremely low base rate: with the event it is predicting happening to about one company in 400, getting 43 correct calls in 100 against that background is a huge improvement, and the one we most strongly present commercially. A newly registered charge is a financing event with a decision behind it, and a company that is about to make that decision is one worth speaking to first for Lloyds.

In terms of how sound the 4 target approach is, we have measured it. Across the 400 rows (4 shortlist, one per target x top 100 companies = 400 rows total) there are 398 unique companies. There are two appearing twice, both on Lending Readiness and Growth Signal, but this is economically sensible given that a company that's growing is exactly the kind of company that also needs to borrow to fund that growth, so the overlap isn't random noise. That is the operational payoff of the decision in \S\ref{sec:targets} to build four separate indices rather than one composite prospect score: the four carve the book into nearly non-overlapping groups rather than re-ranking the same few thousand companies four times.

We need however to flag a limitation here: the voluntary exit ranking should not be treated as a
ranking of individuals. Its top 1000 scores contain only fourteen distinct values, and 138
companies are tied at exactly the score sitting in 100th place, so "the top 100" there is an
arbitrary hundred drawn from a pool of more than two hundred equally ranked companies, and
re-running the sort could return a different hundred with identical scores. In other words, the model can't tell apart the companies at the top of its list; it lumps them into a few big groups with identical scores instead of ranking them one by one. The other three targets do not have this problem: their top 1000 hold 993, 961 and 994 distinct scores, with a single company at the cut.

\subsection{One company, end to end}
Because a ranked list is not the deliverable and a scored, explained row is. Our worked example throughout Chapter 3 is a Small manufacturer, about ten years old, active in all thirty-three panel months. It took on new secured borrowing three separate times inside the window, it was a Lloyds client at the start of the panel and released both Lloyds charges in November 2023 before borrowing from two competitors, and in February 2026 it won its first public-sector contract, a £597k award. Both answers are right about that company, and the reasons behind the first are the ones an RM would actually open with: it already carries charges, it has borrowed repeatedly and recently, and it has just landed a first public contract, which is a textbook working-capital trigger.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
The limitations below are listed plainly without softening. Naming them makes the work more trustworthy. Most of them are recorded openly in the project's own run log.

\paragraph{A permanent gap in the source}
Companies House never published a snapshot for June 2025 \cite{companieshouse_api}. The is a permanent gap, not a download error. The pipeline was built to cope with it, but it still leaves a mark for the month that sits right  next to the gap, most of all a one-month change measured at July 2025, those features comes back blank rather than being guessed.

\paragraph{The growth model's feature count was, for a while, misleading}
A bug in the growth pipeline meant nine  of its features were completely empty in every training month, even though they had values in the test months. A model cannot learn from a column it never sees while training, so the growth model was really  using far fewer features than its label claimed. Those columns were removed, taking the nominal featured from a forty-one featured to twenty-seven it actually uses, and is reported honestly.

\paragraph{Secured lending does not show the full relationship}
Features used in this project show who has registered a loan against a company, but that captures secured borrowing only. It says nothing about current accounts, deposits, unsecured loans or advice, so it is a narrow view of the real relationship. A fuller picture would need informtion the public record does not hold and it is well established that non-financial and behavioral data add real value beyong any single record like this \cite{altman_sabato_wilson_2010,berg_etal_2020}

\paragraph{Limited population}
the modelling data was deliberately not restricted to actively trading firms. It keeps every company status, so that failed companies supply the insolvency labels the model learns from and it includes roughly 181,000 dormant companies rather than dropping them. But the two boundaries remain.The population is filtered to project's three target sectors about 1.5 million companies of the roughly 5.7 million on the register, which is smaller slice of the whole UK business population \cite{beis_bpe}. So the findings are specific to those sectors. Live scores are produced only for active companies because a lending ot growth forecast for a company already is liquidation is meaningless. Extending scoring to a wider deployed population is a natural next step.

This appendix documents the Standard Industrial Classification (SIC)
filtering procedure used to define the analytical population. The purpose is
to make the sector selection and classification rules fully reproducible.

\section{Sector definitions}

The final sector classification consisted of three groups:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Technology, Legal \& Professional}: companies whose SIC
    classification belongs to either Section J (Information and communication)
    or Section M (Professional, scientific and technical activities).

    \item \textbf{Manufacturing}: companies whose SIC classification belongs
    to Section C (Manufacturing).

    \item \textbf{Fast growth \& emerging}: companies with at least one SIC
    code matching one of the specifically selected SIC codes listed in
    Section~\ref{app:sic-specific-codes}.
\end{enumerate}

The classification was applied across all four SIC-code fields available for
each company. A company was assigned to Fast growth \& emerging if at least
one of its four SIC codes matched the specified codes. This category takes
precedence over the broader sector classifications.
